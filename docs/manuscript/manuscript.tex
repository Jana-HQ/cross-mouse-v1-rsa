% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}


When different mice view the same natural image, their primary visual cortex (V1) populations share a measurable fraction of representational structure, yet whether this cross-individual agreement is constant across the visual response or concentrated in a particular moment is unknown. Using millisecond-resolution representational similarity analysis (RSA) of Neuropixels recordings from all 32 \texttt{brain\_observatory\_1.1} sessions of the Allen Brain Observatory (32 mice, 118 natural images), we show that cross-mouse consistency is dynamic: it rises to a transient peak $\sim$100~ms after stimulus onset (Spearman $r = 0.307$) before decaying to a lower sustained floor ($r = 0.182$). This peak coincides precisely with a several-fold collapse in the effective dimensionality of the shared representation, and dimensionality and consistency remain tightly coupled across the entire post-stimulus epoch ($r = -0.929$ on independent, non-overlapping windows). The moment mice agree most is the moment the population code is most compressed. This transient state is specific to naturalistic stimuli --- static gratings produce a flat curve with no transient peak, an effect not attributable to their smaller stimulus set --- and is highest in V1, with lower, roughly comparable consistency across four extrastriate areas.

To probe what produces this compression, we dissociated fast-spiking (FSU, putative inhibitory) from regular-spiking (RSU) units. Individual FSU units carry $\sim$1.9$\times$ the cross-mouse-consistent signal of RSU units (Mann--Whitney $p = 1.4 \times 10^{-45}$), an advantage that survives controlling for firing magnitude and sparsity and that includes a sharper transient shape; optotagged PV$^+$ interneurons behave consistently with this dissociation. A minimal Wilson--Cowan excitatory--inhibitory circuit model, fit to image-selective firing rates and cross-validated on held-out mice, reproduces the RSU/FSU dynamics only in an inhibition-dominated regime ($w_{ee}/w_{ie} < 1$ in every fold). Together, these results show that mouse V1 passes through a transient, low-dimensional state during natural image viewing --- plausibly imposed by fast-spiking inhibition --- and that this state is precisely when representational structure is most shared across individuals.

\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} primary visual cortex; representational
similarity analysis; cross-individual variability; fast-spiking
interneurons; Wilson-Cowan model; neural geometry; Allen Brain
Observatory

% ── Introduction ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

A central goal of systems neuroscience is to identify which features of
neural population activity are canonical --- shared across individuals of
the same species exposed to the same stimuli --- and which are
idiosyncratic, reflecting individual developmental history, anatomy, or
ongoing internal state.  In primary visual cortex, population responses
to natural images vary substantially across individuals in terms of
absolute firing rates and the identity of which neurons are active, yet
downstream circuits must extract a stable visual representation
regardless.  One quantitative handle on this problem is cross-individual
representational consistency: the degree to which different animals agree
on the relational structure of their population responses --- which
stimuli are represented as similar to which others --- even when their
absolute responses differ \citep{kriegeskorte2008}.  High cross-individual
consistency implies that a canonical representational geometry is expressed
in V1, potentially reflecting shared tuning properties that emerge from
common developmental programs and anatomical connectivity.

The geometry of V1 population representations has been characterised in
terms of dimensionality \citep{stringer2019}, the alignment of neural
manifolds across animals \citep{williams2021}, and the degree to which
response structure is predicted by deep convolutional neural network (CNN)
features \citep{yamins2016,cadena2019}.  These studies have generally
treated representational consistency as a static quantity, computed over a
broad temporal window that collapses the entire visual response into a
single point estimate.  However, visual responses in V1 are dynamically
structured: an early feedforward volley arriving at approximately 40--45~ms
post-onset is followed by recurrent and feedback contributions that emerge
around 80~ms and later \citep{lamme2000,kirchberger2023}.  Whether
cross-individual agreement on representational structure is uniformly
distributed across this epoch, or concentrated in a particular temporal
component reflecting a specific circuit mechanism, is unknown.

Here we ask whether the V1 population code passes through a transient,
low-dimensional regime during the visual response, and if so, whether
that is the moment at which different animals' representations are most
alike.  A code transiently compressed into a small number of stereotyped
dimensions is, almost by construction, more likely to look similar across
individuals than a code spread across many idiosyncratic dimensions.
Cross-individual RSA consistency is therefore not only a measure of
agreement in its own right, but a sensitive, model-free readout of when
the population code is in such a compressed, canonical state.

Having established whether and when such a state occurs, we then ask
what produces it.  Fast-spiking interneurons --- the dominant subtype
of which expresses parvalbumin (PV$^+$) --- are a strong mechanistic
candidate.  Putative fast-spiking interneurons in V1 receive strong
thalamic drive and fire with short latency and high spike-timing
precision \citep{zhuang2013,bereshpolova2020}, properties consistent with a role for PV$^+$ cells specifically, which
receive intense monosynaptic thalamocortical drive \citep{cruikshank2007}
and whose visual response properties have been directly characterized in
genetically identified recordings from mouse V1 \citep{runyan2010}, and
provide powerful lateral inhibition onto pyramidal cells through
perisomatic synapses \citep{freund2007}. The classical iceberg effect posits that
inhibition sculpts a broad excitatory input into a sharper, more
selective response \citep{shapiro2022, troyer1998}.  If this inhibitory
sharpening operates at the population level, fast-spiking units should
show a sharper version of any transient compression than broad-spiking
units, and a circuit model in which inhibition dominates recurrent
excitation should reproduce the observed dynamics.

We test these two questions in sequence using the Allen Brain Observatory
Visual Coding Neuropixels dataset \citep{siegle2021}, which provides
simultaneous recordings from hundreds of V1 units across 32 mice viewing
an identical set of 118 natural images.  We first apply a sliding-window
RSA approach at 10~ms temporal resolution to characterise the full
temporal trajectory of cross-individual consistency, and pair it with
participation ratio analysis to test directly whether peak consistency
coincides with minimum dimensionality.  We then turn to mechanism:
waveform-based cell-class dissociation, optotagged PV$^+$ recordings,
and a Wilson-Cowan E/I firing-rate model fit directly to firing rates
and cross-validated on held-out mice.  As mechanistic contrasts, we compare
the temporal
profiles of natural scenes with static gratings and examine how
consistency varies across five visual areas.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Cross-mouse consistency in V1 is substantial, varies across
natural images, and is predicted by response sparsity}

We recorded spike times from VISp units in 32 mice viewing 118 natural
images (250~ms each, 50 repetitions per image) from the Allen Brain
Observatory \textit{brain\_observatory\_1.1} dataset \citep{siegle2021}.
For each session, units passing quality control (amplitude cutoff $< 0.1$,
presence ratio $> 0.9$, ISI violation rate $< 0.5$; $n = 14$--$110$ units
per session after filtering) were used to construct a
$118 \times n_\mathrm{units}$ trial-averaged, column-wise z-scored response
matrix.  Cross-mouse consistency was quantified using representational
similarity analysis (RSA): for each image, we computed the cosine
similarity of its population response vector to all other 117 images
within each mouse, then measured how much mice agreed on this ranking
using the mean Spearman rank correlation across all
$\binom{32}{2} = 496$ mouse pairs.

Within-mouse split-half reliability of the raw response vectors
(Spearman-Brown corrected, 20 random splits) was near unity
(mean $r = 0.978$, range $[0.968, 0.983]$; Figure~\ref{fig:consistency}A),
confirming that the analysis is not power-limited by within-session
trial noise.  This response-vector reliability is not directly comparable
to cross-mouse consistency, however, since the two are computed in
different spaces: reliability here is a per-unit response-vector
correlation, whereas consistency is computed on image~$\times$~image
similarity structure (RDM space).  Computing split-half reliability in
RDM space --- building two similarity matrices from independent trial
halves and correlating them exactly as cross-mouse consistency
correlates two mice's similarity matrices --- gives a within-mouse
RDM-space reliability of mean $r = 0.832$ (range $[0.609, 0.929]$),
which is the number properly comparable to the consistency values
reported below.

Cross-mouse consistency was significantly above zero for all 118 images
(mean $r = 0.314$, range 0.142--0.588;
Figure~\ref{fig:consistency}B).  The fourfold range across images
indicates that some images drive strongly shared representations while
others are encoded more idiosyncratically.

Response sparsity --- the fraction of units responding below the
per-mouse median absolute z-score --- was an image-level predictor of
consistency (Spearman $r = -0.470$,
$p = 7.8 \times 10^{-8}$): images activating fewer units were more
consistently represented across mice.  Response magnitude was a
secondary predictor (Spearman $r = +0.276$, $p = 2.5 \times 10^{-3}$;
Figure~\ref{fig:predictors}A--B).  A ResNet50 CNN feature embedding
also predicted consistency in leave-one-out ridge regression; comparing
across layers, layer~2 (512-dimensional) gave the best accuracy
(LOO Pearson $r = -0.496$, $p = 1.1 \times 10^{-8}$) and was selected as
the primary CNN predictor, with the final layer (1000-dimensional)
performing comparably ($r = -0.486$, $p = 2.5 \times 10^{-8}$;
Figure~\ref{fig:predictors}C).  To ask whether this CNN-based signal was
subsumed by sparsity, we compared nested regression models: adding the
CNN prediction to a sparsity-only model ($R^2 = 0.269$) raised $R^2$ to
0.453 for layer~2 ($\Delta R^2 = +0.184$) and to 0.374 for the final
layer ($\Delta R^2 = +0.105$; Figure~\ref{fig:predictors}D).  This
nested-$R^2$ comparison is the primary evidence that CNN features carry
signal beyond sparsity, since it is a direct model-fit comparison rather
than a residual-alignment statistic.  As a secondary check, added-variable
partial correlation, controlling for sparsity, retained
$r_\mathrm{partial} = -0.501$ for layer~2 and $-0.378$ for the final
layer.  A second, non-parametric partialling method (rank-residualised
Spearman correlation) gave a substantially larger-magnitude partial
correlation for both layers ($r_\mathrm{partial} = -0.828$,
$p = 5.5\times10^{-31}$ for layer~2; $r_\mathrm{partial} = -0.887$,
$p = 9.9\times10^{-41}$ for the final layer; Supplementary
Figure~\ref{fig:S2}C).  This magnitude jump across partialling methods is a
signature of sparsity acting as a partial suppressor --- it shares
variance with both consistency and the CNN prediction, so removing it
from both can inflate residual alignment --- and is a reason for caution
rather than confidence: we treat the partial-correlation values as
illustrative rather than as precise effect sizes, and rely on the
nested $\Delta R^2$ for the headline claim (Methods).  Response
sparsity and CNN-layer features therefore each explain distinct
variance in cross-mouse consistency, and this conclusion is robust to
the specific CNN layer used.  Galleries of the most and least
consistently represented images confirm the sparsity relationship
qualitatively: high-consistency images tend to be sparse, high-contrast,
or dominated by a single feature, while low-consistency images are
denser and more spatially heterogeneous (Supplementary
Figure~\ref{fig:S1}).

\subsection{Cross-mouse consistency follows a stereotyped two-component
temporal trajectory}

To characterise how cross-mouse agreement evolves over the visual
response, we applied the same RSA pipeline to 25~ms sliding windows
stepped at 10~ms increments from $-50$ to $+370$~ms relative to
stimulus onset.  Pre-stimulus consistency was near zero
(mean $r = 4 \times 10^{-5}$), confirming that the measure is not
contaminated by correlated spontaneous activity or slow drift across
sessions (Figure~\ref{fig:temporal}).

Following stimulus onset, consistency rose steeply from $\sim$40~ms,
reaching its first threshold-crossing value at 50~ms and peaking at
$r = 0.307$ at 100~ms.  Consistency then decayed monotonically to a
sustained floor estimated from the 220--250~ms window
($r = 0.182 \pm 0.003$).  We restrict the sustained
floor estimate to the 220--250~ms epoch specifically to avoid
contamination from the next image's neural response: in the
\textit{brain\_observatory\_1.1} protocol, natural scenes are presented
back-to-back with no inter-stimulus interval, so the subsequent image's
V1 response arrives at approximately 290--300~ms (stimulus onset at
250~ms plus $\approx$40--50~ms response latency).  Data beyond 250~ms
are not interpreted as reflecting the sustained representation of the
current image.

A coarser
comparison of early (0--75~ms) and late (75--250~ms) windows confirmed
that late-window consistency substantially exceeds early-window
consistency (mean $r = 0.084$ vs.\ $0.320$; Wilcoxon signed-rank
$p = 4.2 \times 10^{-21}$; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:S3}), while image
rankings were partially preserved across epochs (Spearman $r = 0.652$
between the 100~ms and 250~ms per-image consistency vectors; Supplementary
Figure~\ref{fig:S7}).

\subsection{The transient peak coincides with a collapse of the shared
representation into a low-dimensional attractor}

To characterise the geometry of the shared representation at each time
point, we computed the participation ratio (PR) of the average cosine
similarity matrix across mice:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PR} = \frac{\bigl(\sum_i \lambda_i\bigr)^2}{\sum_i \lambda_i^2},
  \label{eq:PR}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_i$ are the non-negative eigenvalues of the average
similarity matrix.  PR measures effective dimensionality: PR~$= 1$ if one
eigenvalue dominates; PR~$= n$ if all eigenvalues are equal.

Pre-stimulus PR of the cross-mouse-averaged similarity matrix was
$\approx 108$ --- close to the maximum of 118 given the number of
images --- indicating that spontaneous activity produces an
approximately uniform representational geometry with no shared structure
across mice.  Because averaging similarity matrices across mice pushes
disagreement toward uniformity, this averaged-matrix PR is partly
definitional with cross-mouse consistency; a less confounded view is
the PR of each mouse's own similarity matrix, averaged after the fact
(pre-stimulus per-mouse PR $\approx 35.6$), which never involves
cross-mouse averaging.  At the consistency peak (100~ms window), the
averaged-matrix PR dropped to 31.5, meaning the shared representation
occupied only $\approx$27\% of the available dimensions; the per-mouse
PR dropped correspondingly, from $\approx 35.6$ pre-stimulus to 15.3 at
the peak.  PR then partially recovered to 61.2 (averaged-matrix) and
24.4 (per-mouse) at 250~ms, tracking the decay of consistency
(Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}A).  Across all post-stimulus windows, PR and
mean consistency were strongly negatively correlated both for the
averaged-matrix PR (Spearman $r = -0.853$, $p = 3.5 \times 10^{-6}$) and,
essentially as strongly, for the less-confounded per-mouse PR
(Spearman $r = -0.805$, $p = 3.2 \times 10^{-5}$): the moment of maximal
cross-mouse agreement is precisely the moment of maximal representational
compression, and this holds even when the compression is measured
entirely within individual mice.

Because these post-stimulus windows overlap by up to 60\% of their width
(25~ms windows stepped by 10~ms, subsampled to a 20~ms grid for the PR
analysis), the $n = 19$ points entering this correlation are not
independent, and the analytic $p$-values above understate the true
$p$-value (as similarly noted for Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:S7}B).
We assessed this two ways (Methods).  First, a circular-shift permutation
test, which preserves each series' own autocorrelation while destroying
only their mutual alignment, gave $p = 0.053$ for both the
averaged-matrix and per-mouse correlations --- the observed $r$ was more
extreme than every one of the 18 possible shifts, so this is the floor
$p$-value achievable with this few independent windows, not evidence of a
weak effect.  Second, we recomputed the correlation on windows spaced
30~ms apart (one window-width, so no window shares spike-count bins with
its neighbour): this fully independent, 13-point version gave
$r = -0.929$, $p = 4.6 \times 10^{-6}$, confirming the coupling under a
conventional test that needs no autocorrelation correction.

Because PR and consistency are both computed from the same response
matrices, and both are plausibly driven by the stimulus-evoked response
envelope (weak pre-stimulus and late, strong near the transient peak), we
tested whether this coupling is better explained as a shared dependence on
overall response magnitude (Methods).  The
envelope --- mean raw population response per window, independent of the
per-window z-scoring used for RSA --- itself correlated with both
consistency ($r = 0.525$, analytic $p = 0.021$, circular-shift
$p = 0.211$) and PR (avg-matrix: $r = -0.711$, analytic
$p = 6.5\times10^{-4}$, circular-shift $p = 0.053$; per-mouse:
$r = -0.653$, analytic $p = 2.5\times10^{-3}$, circular-shift $p = 0.053$).
Under the autocorrelation-robust test, the envelope--consistency
correlation is no longer distinguishable from chance, while the
PR--envelope correlations remain at the floor $p$-value achievable given
$n = 19$ independent windows; if anything, this weakens
rather than strengthens a common-cause account of the PR--consistency
coupling.  The PR--consistency coupling itself survived partialling out
the envelope nearly unchanged (avg-matrix: partial $r = -0.801$, analytic
$p = 6.5\times10^{-5}$, circular-shift $p = 0.053$; per-mouse: partial
$r = -0.718$, analytic $p = 8.0\times10^{-4}$, circular-shift $p = 0.053$,
vs.\ raw $r = -0.805$), indicating that PR tracks consistency well beyond
what the response envelope predicts on its own.

Eigendecomposition of the average similarity matrix at 100~ms revealed
that the top 8 eigenvectors account for 35.2\% of variance, with a
gradually decaying spectrum (14.75\%, 4.60\%, 3.57\%, 3.21\%, 2.77\%,
2.12\%, 2.07\%, 2.07\% for dimensions 1--8; Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}B).
The two dominant dimensions were interpretable in terms of low-level
image statistics: Dim~1 correlated positively with response magnitude
(Spearman $r = +0.44$) and early CNN feature strength
(layer~1: $r = +0.41$; layer~4: $r = -0.36$), representing a
signal-strength axis; Dim~2 correlated with response sparsity
($r = -0.51$), representing a spatial-scale axis (see Supplementary
Figure~\ref{fig:S5} for the full property heatmap).  Consistent with the
distributed nature of the shared structure, overall cross-mouse
consistency did not strongly load onto any single eigenvector
(maximum $|r| = 0.28$; Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}C).  Finally, we computed
a second-order RSA --- correlating each mouse's full representational
dynamics profile (similarity to all other images across five time
windows) between mouse pairs --- but we stress at the outset that this is
a related re-parameterisation of the same signal rather than an
independent check: it correlates with first-order consistency at
$r = 0.950$ across images (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:S8}C).  Read in
that light, it is reassuring that it behaves as expected, recovering a
mean second-order $r = 0.187$ across images and a temporal peak at exactly
100~ms, matching the first-order peak.

\subsection{Fast-spiking units carry disproportionately more cross-mouse-consistent
signal than regular-spiking units}

To test whether the transient and sustained components of cross-mouse
consistency are differentially produced by distinct cell classes, we
classified VISp units by waveform trough-to-peak duration.  Rather than a
single hand-picked cutoff, we fit a 2-component Gaussian mixture model
(GMM) to log waveform duration across all 3,694 QC-passing VISp units
(fitting in log-space prevents a small tail of long-duration units,
$\sim$1--2.2~ms, likely triphasic/artifact waveforms, from being fit as
its own spurious component instead of the true narrow/broad structure).
The fit recovered component means of 0.263~ms (narrow) and 0.627~ms
(broad).  A unit was classified as putative fast-spiking (FSU) or
regular-spiking (RSU) only if (a) its posterior probability of component
membership exceeded 0.9, excluding the ambiguous gap between the two
modes, and (b) it fell within a symmetric interval capturing 90\% of that
component's own probability mass around its mean (1.645 standard
deviations in log-space), excluding the long tail from being swept into
RSU merely for being broader than FSU.  This yielded a narrow/broad
boundary pair of 0.357~ms and 0.467~ms and classified 634 units as FSU,
2,777 as RSU, and 283 (7.7\%) as ambiguous (mean 11.3 FSU and 49.6 RSU
units per session).

We then asked whether the FSU/RSU dissociation is visible (i) at the
pooled-population level, where response matrices are built by combining
many units within a session, and (ii) at the single-unit level, where each
unit's own response is scored individually against every other session's
population without any pooling or subsampling.  The single-unit approach
sidesteps every unit-count confound that affects the pooled analysis and,
as we show below, provides the more robust evidence.

\subsubsection{Pooled-population sliding-window analysis}

At the pooled-population level, sessions must have at least 15 units of
\textit{both} classes to be included, so RSU and FSU curves are computed
over an identical session set (9 of 32 sessions).  RSU and FSU
subpopulations showed a raw T/S ratio of $1.60\times$ (peak $r = 0.267$,
sustained $r = 0.167$) and $2.12\times$ (peak $r = 0.277$, sustained
$r = 0.131$) respectively.  Because RSU sessions have far more units than
FSU sessions on average, which inflates similarity-matrix signal-to-noise
ratio independently of any genuine cell-type effect, we subsampled each
RSU session's unit pool down to that same session's own FSU count (not a
single count averaged across sessions) over 50 draws.  The matched RSU T/S
ratio ($2.02\times$; draws: mean $2.05\times$, 95\% range
$1.72$--$2.44\times$) narrowed the gap to FSU's $2.12\times$ without
eliminating it, but with only 9 sessions contributing to this comparison,
the pooled-population result alone is not conclusive.  A parallel
session-level analysis, splitting sessions at the median FSU fraction into
high ($n = 16$, mean fraction 0.273, T/S $= 1.82\times$) and low ($n = 16$,
mean fraction 0.157, T/S $= 1.58\times$) groups, showed the same direction
of effect, which persisted after subsampling the low-FSU group to the
high-FSU group's mean unit count (T/S $= 1.69\times$; Supplementary
Figure~\ref{fig:S4}, panel 1).

\subsubsection{Single-unit cross-mouse consistency}

To obtain a confound-free test of the FSU/RSU dissociation, we scored
every QC-passing unit individually: each unit's own length-118 response
vector (0--250~ms window, z-scored) was run through the same
cosine-similarity and rank-transformed profile machinery used for the
pooled analysis, but with that single unit as the entire population for
its session, and its resulting profile compared against every
\textit{other} session's full population profile in turn.  Averaging
these comparisons gives one cross-mouse-consistency score per unit,
with no population pooling, subsampling, or unit-count confound at any
step (Methods).

FSU units ($n = 368$) scored roughly $1.9\times$ higher than RSU units
($n = 1{,}499$) on this measure (FSU median $= 0.087$, mean $= 0.095$; RSU
median $= 0.046$, mean $= 0.052$; Mann-Whitney $p = 1.4\times10^{-45}$;
Figure~\ref{fig:celltypes}B).  Repeating the single-unit scoring for every
sliding-window time point showed that this advantage is not merely a
magnitude effect: FSU units also show a sharper transient-to-sustained
shape (T/S $= 3.62\times$; peak $= 0.120$, sustained $= 0.033$) than RSU
units (T/S $= 2.65\times$; peak $= 0.056$, sustained $= 0.021$;
Figure~\ref{fig:celltypes}D), consistent with the pooled-population
direction of effect but now measured on a per-neuron basis immune to
population-size confounds.

Because FSU and RSU units are known to differ systematically in raw
firing properties, we tested whether this single-unit advantage is fully
explained by response magnitude and sparsity rather than cell-type
identity \textit{per se}.  FSU units had substantially higher raw response
magnitude and lower lifetime sparseness (Rolls--Tovee measure) than RSU
units (both Mann-Whitney $p < 10^{-300}$).  The raw relationship between
cell-class identity and single-unit consistency (Spearman $r = 0.328$,
$p = 4.3\times10^{-48}$) attenuated but remained highly significant after
partialling out both magnitude and sparsity by rank-residualization
(partial $r = 0.116$, $p = 5.4\times10^{-7}$), indicating that the FSU
advantage is not simply a restatement of FSU units firing more or less
sparsely than RSU units.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Optotagged PV$^+$ validation}{Optotagged PV+ validation}}

To validate the waveform-based classification against ground truth, we
identified optotagged interneurons from the five
Pvalb-IRES-Cre/wt$\times$Ai32 sessions in the
\textit{brain\_observatory\_1.1} dataset.  Units were classified as
optotagged PV$^+$ if they met two criteria simultaneously: mean spike
count in the 0--5~ms post-pulse window $> 0.05$ spikes per pulse, and
light-to-baseline ratio $> 2.0$ (where baseline is the 5~ms pre-pulse
window).  The 5~ms response window was chosen to exclude polysynaptic
responses.  This procedure yielded 18 optotagged VISp units across five
sessions; three sessions contributed $\geq 3$ optotagged units (session
IDs: 721123822, 760345702, 797828357; contributing 3, 3, and 10 confirmed
units respectively) and were used for the pooled-population optotagged
curve, while all 18 units (across all five sessions) were scored
individually with the single-unit method.

Under the current (90\%-mass) GMM boundaries, 14 of the 18 optotagged
units fall in the confidently-FSU range, 3 fall in the confidently-RSU
range, and 1 falls in the excluded ambiguous band (sensitivity, PV$^+
\to$ GMM FSU: 78\%; precision, GMM FSU $\to$ PV$^+$: 27\%;
Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:S4}, panels 2--3).  Scored individually with
the single-unit method, optotagged units had a median consistency at the
66th percentile of the waveform-inferred FSU distribution (median $=
0.123$; Figure~\ref{fig:celltypes}B), and their single-unit sliding-window
curve showed a T/S ratio of $3.18\times$ (peak $= 0.133$, sustained
$= 0.042$; Figure~\ref{fig:celltypes}D), between the waveform FSU and RSU
curves.  At the pooled-population level, the same three-session optotagged
group showed a sharper transient (T/S $= 3.59\times$; peak $= 0.223$,
sustained $= 0.062$) than waveform-inferred FSU (T/S $= 2.12\times$),
consistent with the residual RSU/ambiguous contamination in the waveform
classification (3 of 18 optotagged units fall in the confidently-RSU
range) deflating the waveform-based pooled estimate relative to the
genetically confirmed one.

\subsection{A Wilson-Cowan E/I model fit directly to firing rates
reproduces the RSU/FSU dynamics only in an inhibition-dominated regime}

Cross-mouse consistency is an agreement statistic, not a firing rate, so
a rate model's success in reproducing consistency curves does not, on its
own, establish that a rate model's E/I dynamics track anything about
actual population activity.  To address this directly, we fit a
two-population Wilson-Cowan firing-rate model \citep{wilson1972} to
three different targets in turn: the image-selective firing-rate
amplitude of the RSU (E population) and FSU (I population) pools
(computed directly from the raw spike-count tensors), the same
populations' uncorrected total firing rate, and the RSU/FSU temporal
consistency curves used throughout the paper.  Both populations receive a
shared gamma-shaped thalamic drive (onset $\sim$40~ms; shape fixed to
match the observed response latency).  Because RSU pools are much larger
than FSU pools per session (mean 62.6 vs.\ 16.2 units, across the 9
sessions where both classes meet the unit-count threshold), the primary
analysis fits all three targets to RSU subsampled to each session's own
FSU count (50 draws), with a single random subsample per
draw feeding the consistency, total-rate, and selective-amplitude targets
simultaneously so all three describe an identical matched population.
Fits to the full, unsubsampled RSU pool are reported as a validation
check (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:S9}).

The model is described by:
\begin{align}
  \tau_E \dot{E} &= -E + \sigma\!\left(r_\mathrm{EI}\, w_\mathrm{IE}\, E
    - w_\mathrm{IE}\, I + D(t)\right), \label{eq:E} \\
  \tau_I \dot{I} &= -I + \sigma\!\left(w_\mathrm{EI}\, E
    - w_\mathrm{II}\, I + 0.7\, D(t)\right), \label{eq:I}
\end{align}
where $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$ is the sigmoid activation function,
$D(t)$ is a gamma-shaped thalamic drive, $\tau_E = 20$~ms and
$\tau_I = 10$~ms are fixed at biologically motivated values, and the
five free parameters are
$\{r_\mathrm{EI}, w_\mathrm{IE}, w_\mathrm{EI}, w_\mathrm{II},
I_\mathrm{amp}\}$.  The key design choice is the explicit
reparameterisation of $r_\mathrm{EI} = w_\mathrm{EE}/w_\mathrm{IE}$ as
a free parameter (bounded $[0.01, 2.0]$): $r_\mathrm{EI} < 1$ indicates
inhibition-dominated dynamics; $r_\mathrm{EI} > 1$ indicates
recurrence-dominated dynamics.  The absolute scale of $w_\mathrm{IE}$
is a nuisance parameter not well-determined by the data; $r_\mathrm{EI}$
is the primary inference target.

Total firing rate mixes image-selective, stimulus-driven modulation with
non-selective drive shared identically across all images (e.g.\ a broad
contrast/onset transient).  The latter is exactly what per-unit z-scoring
removes before consistency is computed (Methods), so total rate and
consistency are not measuring the same thing even in principle.  We
therefore also computed an image-selective firing-rate amplitude for
each pool: the per-unit standard deviation of firing rate across images,
averaged over the pool, in the same sliding windows used for the
consistency curves.  A non-selective burst identical across images
contributes $\approx 0$ to this quantity, mirroring the z-scoring step,
while remaining a rate-derived measurement rather than a cross-mouse
agreement statistic.

Fit directly to the matched-count image-selective amplitude --- the
primary fit target throughout this section --- optimisation yielded
$r_\mathrm{EI} = 0.620$ ($R^2_\mathrm{RSU} = 0.900$,
$R^2_\mathrm{FSU} = 0.887$; Figure~\ref{fig:eimodel}A), essentially
uniquely identified across 10 random initialisations (mean
$r_\mathrm{EI} = 0.620$, SD $< 0.001$).  Since $r_\mathrm{EI}$ is bounded
$[0.01, 2.0]$ and the fit could in principle have landed anywhere in that
range, the recovered below-unity value is not a foregone conclusion.
Fitting the same target to the full, unsubsampled RSU pool recovered a
near-identical ratio ($r_\mathrm{EI} = 0.623$; Supplementary
Figure~\ref{fig:S9}A), confirming the matched-count and full-pool
analyses agree.

The concern with any five-parameter fit is that the model might absorb an
arbitrary input into the same answer, in which case the recovered
$r_\mathrm{EI}$ would say nothing about the data.  It does not: fitting
the same model to \textit{uncorrected} total firing rate, which retains
the non-selective drive component that per-unit z-scoring removes before
consistency is computed, yields a markedly different and worse-fitting
estimate on both the matched ($r_\mathrm{EI} = 1.112$;
$R^2_\mathrm{RSU} = 0.769$, $R^2_\mathrm{FSU} = 0.456$) and full
($r_\mathrm{EI} = 1.169$) pools.  Fitting the wrong signal therefore
produces a visibly worse and quantitatively different (above-unity,
recurrence-dominated) answer, so the below-unity value recovered from
image-selective amplitude reflects a property of that signal rather than
the model's flexibility.  Sensitivity analysis clarifies why the value
matters: within this model family, a T/S ratio $> 1$ requires
$r_\mathrm{EI} < 1$; increasing $r_\mathrm{EI}$ above 1 flattens the
transient and produces a sustained plateau inconsistent with the data
(Figure~\ref{fig:eimodel}B).  A fit of the same model to the cross-mouse
consistency curves reaches a similar ratio ($r_\mathrm{EI} = 0.641$
matched, $0.651$ full; Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:S9}B), a small
additional validation that the same regime describes the agreement
statistic used elsewhere in the paper, though the two fit targets share
temporal shape and this should not be read as independent corroboration.

To test whether the inhibition-dominance conclusion generalises to
sessions not used in fitting, we performed five-fold cross-validation
over the 9 shared sessions on the matched selective-amplitude fit
(Figure~\ref{fig:eimodel}C), reusing the same underlying per-draw
matched-count subsamples restricted to each fold's sessions rather than
redrawing.  In each fold ($\sim$7--8 training sessions, $\sim$1--2 held
out), the model was fit using three warm starts near the full-data
solution; the fitted parameters were then used to predict the held-out
curve.  Results: mean train $R^2 = 0.878 \pm 0.013$; mean test
$R^2 = 0.828 \pm 0.041$; $r_\mathrm{EI} = 0.621 \pm 0.028$;
$r_\mathrm{EI} < 1$ in every fold (fold-level values: 0.647, 0.599,
0.654, 0.610, 0.594).  The inhibition-dominance conclusion is robust even
in the worst-performing fold (test $R^2 = 0.791$,
$r_\mathrm{EI} = 0.647$).  Because each fold is fit with warm starts near
the full-data solution, this cross-validation demonstrates that the fit
\textit{quality} generalises to held-out sessions; it does not by itself
demonstrate that $r_\mathrm{EI}$ is basin-independent within each fold ---
that evidence comes instead from the 10-random-initialisation stability
analysis above.  With only 9 sessions meeting the unit-count threshold for
both classes, each fold's held-out set is small (1--2 sessions), so the
cross-validation should be read as a consistency check on fit quality
rather than a high-powered generalisation test.

\subsection{Controls: adaptation and behavioural state do not explain
the results}

\paragraph{Adaptation.}
If all mice habituate to the same images in the same way, their responses
might converge over the session through shared adaptation rather than
shared tuning.  To test this, we split each session's trials by temporal
order and computed cross-mouse consistency from the chronologically first
and second halves separately.  Per-image consistency was highly stable
across halves (Spearman $r = 0.960$, $p = 6.1 \times 10^{-66}$;
Figure~\ref{fig:controls}A), and both halves correlated with full-session
consistency at $r > 0.984$ (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:S10}B--C).  The
mean per-image difference (early $-$ late consistency) was a small but
statistically significant $+0.015$ (one-sample $t$-test
$p = 1.1 \times 10^{-8}$) --- in the direction opposite to a shared
adaptation confound, which would predict convergence in the second half
--- consistent instead with modest non-selective neural fatigue that
attenuates all images equally without distorting representational
structure.

\paragraph{Behavioural state.}
If mice happen to be in more similar arousal or locomotion states during
certain images, those images would appear more consistently represented
for reasons unrelated to their visual content.  The coefficient of
variation (CV) of pupil area across mice per image was not significantly
correlated with per-image consistency (Spearman $r = +0.056$, $p = 0.55$,
$n = 26$ sessions with eye-tracking; Figure~\ref{fig:controls}B).  The CV
of running speed was likewise uncorrelated ($r = -0.033$, $p = 0.72$,
$n = 32$ sessions; Figure~\ref{fig:controls}C).  The narrow range of CV
across images (pupil CV SD $\approx 0.014$, running CV SD
$\approx 0.026$) limits statistical power for both tests, and
particularly for the pupil test given its smaller session count
($n = 26$); with this little variation to correlate against, the tests
are underpowered to detect anything but a fairly large true effect, so
the null result is interpreted cautiously rather than as positive
evidence of no relationship.

\subsection{The transient is specific to natural scenes: gratings produce
a flat, still-rising response rather than a transient peak}

Static gratings produced substantially higher absolute consistency than
natural scenes (mean $r = 0.720$ vs.\ $r = 0.314$ for natural scenes in
the fixed 0--250~ms window, both computed with the same single-window RSA
pipeline; Figure~\ref{fig:gratings}A), but the temporal structure differed
qualitatively.  Both stimulus classes have similarly high within-mouse
response reliability (gratings mean $= 0.992$, natural scenes mean $=
0.978$; Figure~\ref{fig:gratings}B; Methods), so this comparison is not
confounded by differential reliability between conditions.  Gratings rose
by $\sim$60~ms and then remained flat for the rest of the 0--250~ms epoch
rather than resolving into a clear plateau-then-hold: the quoted peak
($r = 0.680$) falls at the 250~ms window edge, where the curve is still
drifting slightly upward, giving a near-unity T/S ratio (approximately
$1.00\times$).  Natural scenes showed the characteristic
transient-plus-sustained structure (peak $r = 0.307$ at 100~ms,
T/S $= 1.68\times$).  We restrict quantitative comparisons to the
0--250~ms window for the same contamination reason as natural scenes.

The raw-magnitude gap above could in principle reflect a dimensionality
artefact rather than a genuine representational difference: gratings have
30 conditions (29-dimensional similarity profiles) while natural scenes
have 118 images (117-dimensional profiles), and the per-item Spearman
consistency measure's noise properties depend on this dimensionality
independently of any real representational difference.  To control for
this, we recomputed natural-scenes consistency after subsampling the same
30 images per random draw (50 draws, matching the gratings condition
count exactly), scored in the same single 0--250~ms window
(Figure~\ref{fig:gratings}C).  This left the fixed-window estimate
essentially unchanged, $r = 0.303$ (95\% range across draws:
$[0.258, 0.354]$), against $r = 0.314$ for the full 118-image sample ---
indicating that the raw-magnitude gap is \emph{not} a dimensionality
artefact --- while the item-count-matched value remains far below gratings
($r = 0.720$), with the T/S ratio essentially unchanged under matching
($1.66\times$ vs.\ $1.68\times$ unmatched).  Both the magnitude and shape
comparisons between stimulus classes therefore point to the same
conclusion: the gap reflects a genuine representational difference
between stimulus classes, not an artefact of the unequal item counts.
This control isolates item count specifically; it does not equate the
underlying stimulus geometry (30 gratings tile a smooth
orientation~$\times$~spatial-frequency manifold, whereas 30 random natural
images scatter across a higher-dimensional space), which is itself part of
the representational difference being characterised.

This comparison is consistent with the view that feedforward responses
to simple, low-complexity stimuli are sufficient to produce stable shared
representations in V1, while the complex stimulus-specific inhibitory
sharpening mechanism is only recruited for natural images that engage a
broader, less stereotyped neural population.

\subsection{Cross-area hierarchy: consistency and onset latency vary with
hierarchical position}

We applied the same sliding-window pipeline to four additional visual
areas (VISl, VISal, VISpm, VISam; session counts 24, 23, 20, 26
respectively, with $\geq 10$ QC-passing units per session).  VISp had the
highest peak consistency ($r = 0.307$) and earliest onset (50~ms, peak at
100~ms).  In a fixed 0--250~ms window, VISp likewise showed the highest
mean consistency ($r = 0.314$) of the five areas (VISl $r = 0.202$,
VISal $r = 0.249$, VISpm $r = 0.244$, VISam $r = 0.261$), all computed
with the same single-window RSA pipeline.  The four extrastriate areas
showed later onsets and lower, roughly comparable peak consistency rather
than a monotonic decline with hierarchical position: VISl $r = 0.186$
(onset 60~ms, peak 110~ms), VISal $r = 0.234$ (onset 60~ms, peak 100~ms),
VISpm $r = 0.210$ (onset 70~ms, peak 120~ms), VISam $r = 0.238$
(onset 70~ms, peak 110~ms; Figure~\ref{fig:hierarchy}).  All five areas
have similarly high within-mouse response reliability (range
0.964--0.978; Methods), so this ordering reflects genuine differences in
raw consistency rather than differential reliability.  The
transient-plus-sustained temporal structure was qualitatively present in
all areas.  Between-area comparisons are reported descriptively; areas
share sessions, and a formal test would require a mixed model accounting
for this dependence.

The five areas also differ in mean per-session unit count (VISp 63.0,
VISl 38.9, VISal 67.1, VISpm 44.0, VISam 57.9), which inflates
similarity-matrix SNR and hence raw T/S ratio independently of any
genuine areal difference.  To control for this, we randomly subsampled
every area's unit pool down to 39 units/session (VISl's mean, the
smallest of the five) over 20 draws and recomputed T/S on each matched
draw.  Matched T/S ratios were uniformly higher than raw (e.g.\ VISp:
$1.68\times \to 1.81\times$; VISl: $2.03\times \to 2.13\times$; full
values in Methods), and the ranking was preserved for three of the five
areas (VISl $>$ VISam $>$ VISal, both raw and matched), but VISp and
VISpm swapped order: VISpm was marginally higher than VISp under the raw
comparison ($1.74\times$ vs.\ $1.68\times$) but marginally lower under
the matched comparison ($1.79\times$ vs.\ $1.81\times$).  This pair is
therefore not distinguishable once unit count is controlled, and the
overall cross-area ranking should be read as robust except for the
VISp/VISpm comparison specifically.

\section{Discussion}
 
We have shown that mouse V1 does not represent natural images with a
constant degree of cross-individual agreement. Instead, it passes through a
transient, low-dimensional population state --- peaking at $\sim$100~ms
post-stimulus and visible directly in the participation ratio of the shared
representation --- and this transient state is precisely the moment at which
different animals' representations are most alike. This is the strongest and
most direct result in the paper, requiring no cell-type classification,
circuit model, or auxiliary assumptions: the temporal profile is incompatible
with either purely feedforward tuning (a step function) or purely recurrent
attractor dynamics (a sustained plateau), and its peak coincides with a
collapse of the shared geometry to its minimum dimensionality (participation
ratio $= 31.5$ at 100~ms, against a pre-stimulus baseline of $\approx 108$).
Because both quantities come from the same similarity matrices,
cross-individual agreement is high exactly when the population code is
compressed into a small number of stereotyped dimensions --- in the language
of neural population geometry \citep{chung2021}, stimulus onset transiently
contracts the representational space rather than adding a new, image-specific
direction to it. Such contraction is not specific to vision or to this
particular statistic: comparable stimulus-triggered reductions in the
dimensionality or trial-to-trial variability of population activity have been
reported across cortical areas, species, and recording modalities
\citep{churchland2010, mazzucato2016}.
 
Three features make this coupling robust rather than definitional. It holds
when the participation ratio is computed independently within each mouse and
only then averaged (per-mouse PR: pre-stimulus $\approx 35.6 \to$ peak 15.3;
Spearman $r = -0.805$), ruling out the concern that the averaged-matrix result
simply restates consistency. It survives partialling out a proxy for the
shared stimulus-evoked response envelope (partial $r = -0.801$ avg-matrix,
$-0.718$ per-mouse), even as the raw envelope--consistency link itself fails
to survive an autocorrelation-robust test. And it holds on non-overlapping
windows that share no spike-count bins, independent by construction ($r =
-0.929$, $p = 4.6 \times 10^{-6}$). The existence and timing of this transient
state, and its coupling to consistency, is therefore secure independently of
the mechanistic claims that follow.
 
The remaining results address why this compression occurs, and converge on
fast-spiking inhibition. Scored one neuron at a time, individual FSU units
carry roughly $1.9\times$ the cross-mouse-consistent signal of RSU units
(median $0.087$ vs.\ $0.046$; Mann-Whitney $p = 1.4 \times 10^{-45}$),
surviving controls for raw magnitude and sparsity (partial $r = 0.116$,
$p = 5.4 \times 10^{-7}$) and including a sharper single-unit transient shape
(T/S $3.62\times$ vs.\ $2.65\times$). Interneuron physiology offers converging
reasons to expect exactly this pattern: parvalbumin-expressing interneurons
pool broadly across the local excitatory population, tracking the net tuning
bias of their neighbours rather than a sharp, private receptive field, so an
averaged signal should by default vary less across animals; they impose a
largely divisive, gain-like transformation on pyramidal output, consistent
with the iceberg-style sharpening already invoked to explain the transient's
shape; and their recruitment, some 50--60~ms after the initial
$\sim$40--45~ms feedforward volley, is closer to the timescale of feedback
inhibition --- recruited only once local principal neurons begin firing ---
than to that earliest feedforward wave itself \citep{kerlin2010, atallah2012,
shapiro2022, troyer1998, lamme2000, kirchberger2023, isaacson2011}. A recent
preprint applying an analogous consistency logic to the same 118-image
natural-scenes stimulus set, recorded via two-photon imaging across
genetically-defined Cre lines, similarly finds inhibitory (PV, SST, VIP)
population geometry clustering by cell-type identity almost independent of
visual area, unlike excitatory geometry \citep{toosi2025}.
 
Within our own data, this dissociation is corroborated by pooled-population
and optotagging analyses: raw pooled T/S is $2.12\times$ (FSU) vs.\ $1.60\times$
(RSU, $2.02\times$ matched for unit count), and genetically confirmed PV$^+$
units, scored individually, fall at the 66th percentile of the
waveform-inferred FSU distribution with an even sharper pooled transient (T/S
$3.59\times$) --- consistent with the waveform classifier's known, imperfect
agreement with optotagging (sensitivity $78\%$), itself typical of
duration-based interneuron classification generally \citep{ye2025}. Finally,
because cross-mouse consistency is an agreement statistic and not a firing
rate, we fit a Wilson-Cowan E/I model directly to firing rates. Fit to
matched-count image-selective firing-rate amplitude, the model generalises to
held-out sessions (five-fold cross-validation, mean test $R^2 = 0.828 \pm
0.041$) and recovers a below-unity, inhibition-dominated connectivity ratio
($r_\mathrm{EI} = w_\mathrm{EE}/w_\mathrm{IE} = 0.621 \pm 0.028$, below 1 in
every fold); fit instead to uncorrected total firing rate, which retains the
non-selective drive that z-scoring removes before consistency is computed,
the same model returns a markedly worse, recurrence-dominated estimate
($r_\mathrm{EI} = 1.112$), and within this model family the T/S ratio itself
requires $r_\mathrm{EI} < 1$. This inhibition-dominated regime is the
operating point identified by a body of work on stabilized supralinear
networks, in which sufficiently strong recurrent excitation requires feedback
inhibition for stability and the degree of this stabilisation governs
response sharpness, gain, and the stimulus-driven quenching of shared
population variability, including when comparable models are fit directly to
spiking recordings from mouse cortex \citep{ahmadian2013, rubin2015,
hennequin2018, stringer2016}. We emphasise, however, that our central
empirical claim does not depend on the correctness of any circuit-level
interpretation.
 
Our findings extend, rather than contradict, existing accounts of V1
population geometry. The high-dimensional spontaneous state we observe
(pre-stimulus per-mouse PR $\approx 35.6$) matches the power-law eigenspectrum
reported for V1 \citep{stringer2019}, and a bias-corrected re-analysis of
that same dataset recovers a similarly small number of dominant dimensions
using an independent estimator ($\sim$10 modes, $\sim$30\% of variance,
versus our 8 eigenvectors and 35.2\% at the consistency peak, though the two
are not directly comparable given the different underlying matrices)
\citep{pospisil2025}. Decomposing a visual response by time, rather than
collapsing it into one window, reveals structure invisible to static
approaches --- a lesson also drawn from time-resolved RSA of human MEG, which
dissociates an early, transient representational component from a later,
more persistent one \citep{cichy2014}. Our own RSA approach differs from
Procrustes alignment and hyperalignment \citep{williams2021, haxby2011} in
measuring agreement on representational \textit{structure} rather than the
orientation of neural axes, a distinction that, together with a within-mouse
RDM-space reliability of $r = 0.832$ against a mean cross-mouse consistency of
$r = 0.314$, indicates that a substantial share of the representational
signal is shared across individuals. Finally, the feedforward/recurrent
distinction \citep{lamme2000} maps naturally onto our two-component profile:
the $\sim$100~ms transient falls within the early feedforward epoch, when
shared thalamic-driven tuning should be more stereotyped across individuals
than recurrent contributions shaped by individual experience.
 
The transient's absence for static gratings --- despite their higher absolute
consistency ($r = 0.720$ vs.\ $0.314$, confirmed by item-count matching not
to be a dimensionality artefact) but near-immediate plateau (T/S
$\approx 1.00\times$) rather than a transient-plus-sustained profile (T/S
$= 1.68\times$) --- fits a broader literature in which sparsification and
decorrelation in V1 are engaged by natural-scene statistics specifically,
demonstrated via nonclassical receptive-field manipulations in macaque V1,
movie-versus-control comparisons in mouse V1, and parametric removal of
natural-scene spatial correlations in mouse V1 \citep{vinje2000,
froudarakis2014, rikhye2015}. It is the specific statistical structure of
natural images, not stimulus complexity in some more general sense, that
appears to recruit the compression we observe; gratings, though densely and
reliably encoded, may simply not engage the mechanism.
 
\subsection{Limitations}
 
The Wilson-Cowan model is phenomenological: the fitted connectivity ratio and
the empirical T/S ratio move in near-lockstep, so ``inhibition-dominated'' is
close to a model-based restatement of ``a transient exists'' rather than an
independent synaptic measurement, and it rests on only 9 sessions and 18
optotagged units, so both should be read as directional support rather than
precise estimates. Nor is our $r_\mathrm{EI}$ ratio, a fit to firing rates,
the kind of direct causal test --- e.g.\ the paradoxical suppression of
inhibitory firing under optogenetic drive --- used to establish
inhibition-stabilisation in the formal sense \citep{ahmadian2021}; such tests
do exist for mouse V1, S1, and motor cortex and find clear evidence for it
even at rest, offering a plausible resolution for an earlier report of
non-paradoxical PV responses in V1 layer 2/3 \citep{sanzeni2020, atallah2012}:
stimulating only a genetically incomplete PV subset can mask a
population-level paradoxical signature that is nonetheless genuinely present.
Our fit is best read as weaker, firing-rate-level evidence consistent with,
rather than equivalent to, this more direct line of work.
 
The cell-type statistics carry a related caveat: the single-unit tests treat
1,867 FSU/RSU units as independent despite their nesting within 32 sessions,
so the reported $p$-values are better read as upper bounds on precision than
exact values. The pooled-population comparison is only partially resolved for
the residual unit-count confound between classes, which is why we treat the
confound-free single-unit measure --- itself coarser, comparing one unit
against another session's full population profile rather than a matched
neuron --- as the primary evidence. And controlling for magnitude and
sparsity cannot exhaustively rule out some other correlated firing property
behind the residual FSU/RSU difference.
 
Two features of the design further bound interpretation. The sustained floor
($r = 0.182$, 220--250~ms) comes from a narrow window forced by the
back-to-back presentation protocol, so early contamination from the next
image cannot be fully excluded. And the cross-area comparisons share sessions
and so are not statistically independent; the VISp/VISpm ordering
specifically is not distinguishable once unit count is matched. Finally, the
two dominant attractor dimensions explain only 19.3\% of the shared variance,
and the remainder is not accounted for by the image statistics examined here.
 
Taken together, these findings establish the transient compression of the V1
population code as a measurable, reproducible, and --- in our data ---
mechanistically interpretable property of the visual response, with a
circuit mechanism that the cross-validated E/I model suggests is a stable
property of the mouse V1 architecture rather than an idiosyncrasy of the 32
mice sampled. If the shared geometry of V1 is transiently imposed by
inhibitory interneurons during a 50--150~ms window after stimulus onset,
downstream areas reading out V1 during this window receive a cleaner, more
stereotyped signal than at other times; such temporal gating of canonical
representational structure may be a general principle of cortical
computation, specifically engaged by complex, naturalistic stimuli rather
than by visual stimulation as such (the transient is absent for static
gratings and lower in extrastriate areas than in VISp, though the drop across
extrastriate areas is not itself a graded gradient). A recent review of
response sub-additivity and variability quenching in visual cortex closes by
proposing population-level geometry as the natural next extension of exactly
this literature \citep{goris2024}; the present results, linking a moment of
dimensionality collapse to a moment of maximal cross-individual agreement,
are one instance of what that extension can look like.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Dataset}

All data were obtained from the Allen Brain Observatory Visual Coding
Neuropixels dataset \citep{siegle2021}, accessed via the AllenSDK
(version~2.x).  We used all 32 sessions of type
\textit{brain\_observatory\_1.1}, in which head-fixed mice passively
viewed a standardised battery of natural and artificial stimuli while six
Neuropixels probes recorded simultaneously from multiple visual areas.
The natural scenes stimulus (118 greyscale images, 250~ms per image, 50
repetitions per image, back-to-back with no inter-stimulus interval) and
the static gratings stimulus (orientation $\times$ spatial-frequency
conditions, 250~ms per presentation) were used.  All analyses used the
VISp area designation from the Allen Common Coordinate Framework~v3 for
the main analysis; the cross-area analysis additionally included VISl,
VISal, VISpm, and VISam.  In the \textit{brain\_observatory\_1.1} dataset
there is one session per mouse, so ``32 mice'' and ``32 sessions'' are
used interchangeably throughout, and sessions are the resampling/held-out
unit in the cluster bootstrap and cross-validation procedures described
below.

\subsection{Unit quality control}

Units were retained if they met all three Allen Brain Observatory
recommended quality criteria: amplitude cutoff $< 0.1$, presence ratio
$> 0.9$, and ISI violation rate $< 0.5$.  Sessions were included in the
analysis of a given area only if they contributed at least 10 QC-passing
units in that area.  All 32 sessions contributed at least 10 VISp units
(range: 14--110).  Session counts for other areas were: VISl 24,
VISal 23, VISpm 20, VISam 26.  Mean units/session were: VISp 63.0,
VISl 38.9, VISal 67.1, VISpm 44.0, VISam 57.9.  To control for the
resulting SNR confound in cross-area T/S comparisons (Results), each
area's unit pool was randomly subsampled down to 39 units/session (VISl's
mean, the smallest of the five) over 20 draws, and the sliding
consistency curve and T/S ratio were recomputed on each matched draw.
Raw vs.\ matched T/S ratios were: VISp $1.68\times \to 1.81\times$
(95\% range across draws $1.73$--$1.89\times$), VISl
$2.03\times \to 2.13\times$ ($1.97$--$2.30\times$), VISal
$1.84\times \to 1.92\times$ ($1.84$--$2.00\times$), VISpm
$1.74\times \to 1.79\times$ ($1.72$--$1.87\times$), VISam
$2.00\times \to 2.10\times$ ($1.99$--$2.23\times$).

\subsection{Preprocessing and response matrix construction}

Spike times were binned into 1~ms bins spanning $-75$ to $+404$~ms
relative to stimulus onset and stored as a tensor of shape
(images $\times$ units $\times$ bins) per session.  Any response window
$[t_\mathrm{lo}, t_\mathrm{hi})$~ms was reconstructed by summing the
relevant bin columns, then z-scoring per unit (across images).  This
single-pass preprocessing avoids redundant NWB reads across analyses.
For the main 0--250~ms analysis, z-scored matrices were computed
directly from the summed tensor columns.

\subsection{Within-mouse reliability}

Within-mouse split-half reliability was estimated by randomly partitioning
each image's 50 trials into two halves, computing the mean population
response vector for each half, and measuring their Spearman rank
correlation.  This was repeated 20 times and averaged.  The Spearman-Brown
formula $r_\mathrm{SB} = 2r/(1+r)$ \citep{spearman1910,brown1910} estimated full-data reliability.  This
response-vector reliability (mean of $r_\mathrm{SB}$ across images and
mice) is a QC diagnostic confirming the analysis is not power-limited by
within-session trial noise, but it is not in the same space as
cross-mouse consistency and should not be used to normalise it: it is
computed from raw per-unit response vectors, whereas consistency is
computed on image~$\times$~image similarity structure (RDM space).

To obtain a reliability estimate genuinely comparable to consistency, we
additionally computed an RDM-space split-half reliability for VISp
natural scenes, the one area/stimulus combination for which per-trial
spike data was available.  Each mouse's trials were split in half (20
random splits), each half was converted to a response matrix and then to
a cosine similarity matrix (identical to the main RSA pipeline below),
and the two resulting per-mouse similarity matrices were Spearman
rank-correlated against each other exactly as the per-image consistency
computation correlates two different mice's similarity matrices, then
Spearman-Brown corrected and averaged over splits and mice.  This
RDM-space reliability (mean $r = 0.832$) is the number reported alongside
consistency values in Results.  It was not computed for the four
extrastriate areas or for static gratings because per-trial spike data
for those conditions was not retained in the cached preprocessing
archive; area and stimulus-type comparisons therefore use raw consistency
values throughout rather than a normalised percentage, since both
response-vector reliability (0.964--0.992 across all areas and stimulus
types) and, where computable, RDM-space reliability are high and broadly
comparable across the conditions being compared.

\subsection{Representational similarity analysis and cross-mouse
consistency}

For each session, the inter-image cosine similarity matrix was computed
from the z-scored response matrix:
$S_{ij} = \mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_j /
(\|\mathbf{x}_i\| \|\mathbf{x}_j\| + \epsilon)$,
$\epsilon = 10^{-8}$.  Per-image cross-mouse consistency was the mean
Spearman rank correlation, across all mouse pairs $(a,b)$, between their
row-wise similarity profiles for each image \citep{kriegeskorte2008}.

Cluster bootstrap confidence intervals resampled whole mice (not pairs)
with replacement for 1000 iterations.  When a mouse appeared multiple
times in a bootstrap sample, pairs consisting of two copies of the same
original mouse were excluded, preventing spurious self-correlation from
inflating bootstrap consistency estimates.

\subsection{CNN embeddings}

ResNet50 \citep{he2016} pretrained on ImageNet (IMAGENET1K\_V2)
\citep{deng2009} was used as a feature
extractor; global average pooling was applied to each convolutional block
to yield a fixed-length embedding per image.  Ridge regression
\citep{hoerl1970} with
leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO, 118 images) was used because the
feature dimension (256--2048) greatly exceeds the number of images.
Layer~2 (512-dimensional) was selected as the primary CNN predictor on
the basis of LOO accuracy across layers (Figure~\ref{fig:predictors}C);
the final layer (1000-dimensional), which performed comparably, was
carried through the same sparsity-comparison analysis as a
robustness check (Figure~\ref{fig:predictors}D, Supplementary
Figure~\ref{fig:S2}).

Whether the CNN-based signal added explanatory power beyond sparsity
was assessed primarily by a nested $R^2$ comparison: $R^2$ for
consistency~$\sim$~sparsity alone was compared against
consistency~$\sim$~sparsity~$+$~CNN, where the CNN term was the
full-model ridge LOO prediction (Figure~\ref{fig:predictors}D). This
model-comparison statistic was treated as the headline evidence because,
unlike a partial correlation, it does not require residualising a
fitted, cross-validated prediction against the variable it was fit to
predict.

Two partialling methods were additionally computed as a secondary,
robustness check, reported in Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:S2}.
(1)~\textit{Added-variable (OLS) partial correlation}: consistency,
sparsity, and the full-model CNN LOO prediction were each residualised
by ordinary least-squares regression on the other predictor, and the
Pearson correlation of the residuals gives the partial correlation in
each direction (sparsity~$|$~CNN and CNN~$|$~sparsity).
(2)~\textit{Rank-residualised (Spearman) partial correlation}, an
independent, non-parametric check reported in Supplementary
Figure~\ref{fig:S2}C: both variables were rank-transformed, the linear
effect of the rank-transformed covariate was regressed out, and the
Spearman correlation of the residuals was computed.  In both cases, the
CNN quantity partialled was the full ridge LOO prediction from the
512-dimensional layer~2 (or 1000-dimensional final-layer) embedding, not
a scalar summary of the embedding.  Because these partial-correlation
estimates residualise a prediction that was itself cross-validated
against consistency, and because their magnitude varied substantially
between the two partialling methods (Results), they are reported as
illustrative robustness detail rather than as the primary test of
independence.

\subsection{Sliding-window temporal analysis}

The RSA pipeline was repeated in 25~ms windows stepped at 10~ms
increments from $-50$ to $+370$~ms, reconstructed from the binned tensor.
Response matrices were z-scored independently within each window.  The
sustained floor was estimated from the 220--250~ms window only, to avoid
contamination from the subsequent image's V1 response.  The onset
threshold was defined as the first window where mean consistency exceeded
$\max(2 \times \text{pre-stimulus baseline},\, 0.05)$.

\subsection{Participation ratio}

The PR of a positive semi-definite matrix $\mathbf{M}$ with eigenvalues
$\lambda_i \geq 0$ is $\mathrm{PR}(\mathbf{M}) =
(\sum_i \lambda_i)^2 / \sum_i \lambda_i^2$ \citep{gao2017}.  At each time window,
the average similarity matrix was formed by summing per-session cosine
similarity matrices and dividing by the number of sessions.  The PR
analysis was computed on a 20~ms grid (every other sliding-window step) to
halve computation; both the averaged-matrix PR and the mean of the
per-session PRs were tracked at each window.

\subsection{Response-envelope control}

Because both PR and cross-mouse consistency are computed from the same
per-window response matrices, we tested whether their covariation across
time is attributable to a shared response envelope rather than a specific
link between dimensionality and agreement.  The envelope was defined as
the mean raw (pre-z-score) population response --- summed spike count per
unit per image within each window, averaged over units, images, and mice
--- computed upstream of the per-window z-scoring used for RSA, since that
normalisation removes each window's overall response magnitude by
construction.  Given this envelope $z$, the relationship between
consistency $x$ and PR $y$ was re-assessed with the Spearman partial
correlation
\begin{equation}
  r_{xy \cdot z} = \frac{r_{xy} - r_{xz} \, r_{yz}}
  {\sqrt{(1 - r_{xz}^2)(1 - r_{yz}^2)}},
  \label{eq:partial}
\end{equation}
with analytic significance assessed via a $t$-test on $n - 3$ degrees of
freedom, and with an autocorrelation-robust circular-shift $p$-value
computed as described below.

\subsection{Autocorrelation-robust significance for sliding-window correlations}

Because the sliding windows overlap (25~ms windows stepped by 10~ms, or
20~ms as subsampled for the PR analysis), correlations computed across
post-stimulus windows do not have $n$ independent observations, and the
analytic $p$-values reported alongside them understate the true
$p$-value.  We assessed the PR--consistency and envelope correlations
with two additional, assumption-appropriate tests.

First, a circular-shift permutation test: the null distribution was built
by circularly shifting one series relative to the other by every one of
the $n-1$ non-trivial lags, which preserves each series' own
autocorrelation structure while destroying only their mutual alignment;
the $p$-value is the fraction of shifted correlations at least as extreme
as the observed one.  For the partial correlations, consistency was
shifted relative to the paired (PR, envelope), so the PR--envelope
relationship was preserved under the null.  With $n = 19$ post-stimulus
windows this test has only 18 possible shifts, giving a resolution floor
of $p \approx 1/19 = 0.053$; several of our correlations were more
extreme than all 18 shifts and are reported at this floor rather than as
non-significant.

Second, a non-overlapping-window control: PR and consistency were
recomputed at windows spaced one full window-width (30~ms) apart, so no
two windows share any spike-count bins and the resulting points are
independent by construction.  This yields fewer points (13 post-stimulus
windows) but permits a conventional analytic significance test with no
autocorrelation correction.

\subsection{Waveform-based cell class classification}

Units were classified as FSU (fast-spiking, putative PV$^+$) or RSU
(regular-spiking, putative pyramidal/excitatory) using trough-to-peak
waveform duration, following the canonical narrow/broad distinction
\citep{bartho2004,mccormick1985}, but via a two-threshold rule rather
than the single hand-picked cutoff used in that convention.  A
2-component Gaussian mixture model \citep{trainito2019} was fit to log waveform duration
across all 3,694 QC-passing VISp units (log-space fitting prevents a
small tail of long-duration, likely triphasic/artifact units from being
fit as a spurious third mode).  A unit was assigned to a class only if
(i)~its posterior probability of membership in the corresponding
component exceeded 0.9, and (ii)~it fell within a symmetric interval
capturing 90\% of that component's own probability mass around its mean
(1.645 standard deviations in log-space); units failing either criterion
were labelled ambiguous and excluded from both classes.  This yielded a
narrow/broad boundary pair of 0.357~ms and 0.467~ms, classifying 634
units as FSU, 2,777 as RSU, and 283 (7.7\%) as ambiguous.  For the
pooled-population sliding-window analysis, sessions with fewer than 15
QC-passing units of \textit{both} classes were excluded, so RSU and FSU
curves were computed over an identical session set (9/32 sessions).  To
control for the resulting within-session unit-count difference, each RSU
session's unit pool was also subsampled down to that same session's own
FSU count (rather than a single count averaged across sessions) over 50
random draws, and the sliding consistency curve and T/S ratio were
recomputed on each matched draw (Results).

\subsection{Single-unit consistency}

To measure cross-mouse consistency at the level of individual neurons
rather than pooled populations, each QC-passing unit's response was
treated as an ``$n=1$'' population: its length-118, per-image response
vector (0--250~ms window, or the corresponding sliding window for the
temporal analysis) was z-scored across images and converted to a cosine
similarity matrix in the same way as the pooled response matrices.  This
single-unit similarity matrix was rank-transformed and mean-centered
per image (the same profile representation used internally by the
cross-mouse consistency computation for pooled populations) and compared
against every \textit{other} session's full population profile in turn,
using the same rank-correlation machinery.  Averaging across all other
sessions (and, for the scalar summary, across images) gives one
cross-mouse-consistency score per unit, with no pooling, subsampling, or
within-session unit-count confound at any step.  This procedure was
applied to all FSU/RSU units for a fixed 0--250~ms window and, separately,
at every sliding-window time point to build per-class temporal curves;
optotagged PV$^+$ units were scored individually with the same method.

For the magnitude/sparsity confound check, each unit's raw (non-z-scored)
spike count was summed per image over the same window to obtain a raw
response vector $r$.  Response magnitude was its mean, and lifetime
sparseness was computed as $S = \left(1 - \bar r^2 / \overline{r^2}\right)
/ \left(1 - 1/N\right)$ (Rolls--Tovee measure), where $N = 118$ is the
number of images.  Partial Spearman correlation between cell-class
identity (FSU $=1$, RSU $=0$) and single-unit consistency, controlling for
both magnitude and sparsity jointly, was computed by rank-transforming
all variables, regressing the rank-transformed class indicator and the
rank-transformed consistency score separately on the rank-transformed
covariates via ordinary least squares, and correlating the two sets of
residuals.

\subsection{Optotagging validation}

Genetically confirmed PV$^+$ units were identified from the five
Pvalb-IRES-Cre/wt$\times$Ai32(RCL-ChR2(H134R)\_EYFP)/wt \citep{madisen2012}
sessions in
the \textit{brain\_observatory\_1.1} dataset, using optotagging
\citep{lima2009,cardin2010}.  Single light pulse epochs
were used.  QC-passing VISp units were classified as optotagged PV$^+$
if: (i)~mean spike count in the 0--5~ms post-pulse window $> 0.05$ spikes
per pulse; and (ii)~light-to-baseline ratio $> 2.0$, where baseline was
the mean spike count in the 5~ms pre-pulse window.  The 5~ms response
window excludes polysynaptic responses (pyramidal cells driven by PV$^+$
firing respond at 7--15~ms latency).  Sessions with fewer than 3
optotagged VISp units were excluded from the cross-mouse RSA (sessions
746083955 and 773418906 contributed one unit each).

\subsection{E/I model}

The two-population Wilson-Cowan model \citep{wilson1972} is described by
equations~\eqref{eq:E}--\eqref{eq:I}.  The thalamic drive $D(t)$ is a
gamma-shaped function (shape parameter $k = 5$, scale $\theta = 14$~ms)
with onset at 40~ms, normalised to unit amplitude.  Time constants were
fixed at $\tau_E = 20$~ms and $\tau_I = 10$~ms.  The five free
parameters are $\{r_\mathrm{EI}, w_\mathrm{IE}, w_\mathrm{EI},
w_\mathrm{II}, I_\mathrm{amp}\}$ with bounds $r_\mathrm{EI} \in
[0.01, 2.0]$, $w_\mathrm{IE} \in [0.1, 20.0]$,
$w_\mathrm{EI} \in [0.1, 15.0]$, $w_\mathrm{II} \in [0.0, 8.0]$,
$I_\mathrm{amp} \in [0.5, 20.0]$.  The model was integrated with
\texttt{scipy.integrate.odeint} (rtol $= 10^{-6}$, atol $= 10^{-8}$)
and fit by minimising the sum of mean squared errors between
normalised model outputs and normalised RSU/FSU curves simultaneously
using L-BFGS-B \citep{byrd1995}, with ten initialisations (one informed, nine random) per
fit target.  The model was fit independently to three targets: (i)
cross-mouse consistency curves, (ii) uncorrected total firing rate ---
the mean per-unit, per-image spike rate (Hz) in each sliding window,
summed across the pool --- and (iii) image-selective firing-rate
amplitude, the per-unit standard deviation of firing rate across images
(Hz), averaged over the pool, in the same sliding windows.  Rate curves
(ii) and (iii) were computed directly from the raw spike-count tensors
using the same RSU/FSU unit classification and the 9 sessions
where both classes meet the unit-count threshold, at the same window
grid as the consistency curves.

For the primary (matched-count) analysis, each RSU session's unit pool
was subsampled down to that same session's own FSU count, over 50 draws;
a single random subsample per draw fed the consistency, total-rate, and
selective-amplitude computations simultaneously, so all three targets
were computed from an identical matched population for that draw.  The
resulting per-draw curves were averaged to give the matched-count
consistency, total-rate, and selective-amplitude targets used for the
primary model fits.  FSU pools were never subsampled.  The full,
unsubsampled RSU pool was fit to the same three targets as a validation
check (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:S9}).  Sensitivity to the
$w_\mathrm{II}$ upper bound, shown for all three matched-count fit
targets, is in Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:S9}D.

\subsection{Cross-validation}

Five-fold cross-validation was performed over the 9 shared sessions, for
the matched selective-amplitude fit.  Per-draw, per-session matched-count
curves from the primary analysis (above) were averaged over just each
fold's training or test sessions to build that fold's matched train/test
curves, reusing the same underlying random subsamples rather than
redrawing.  In each fold, the model was fit to the training curves using
three warm starts near the full-data matched solution, and the fitted
parameters were used to predict the held-out curve.  Train and test
$R^2$ were computed on amplitude-normalised curves.

\subsection{Grating response matrices}

Grating conditions were defined as unique (orientation, spatial frequency)
pairs; only conditions present in all 32 sessions (intersection:
30~conditions) were retained.  The same QC thresholds, z-scoring
procedure, and RSA pipeline were applied as for natural scenes.  For the
item-count control, natural-scenes consistency was recomputed after
randomly subsampling to 30 images per draw (50 draws), matching the
gratings condition count, both for the fixed 0--250~ms window and across
the full sliding-window profile.

\subsection{Statistical tests}

Spearman rank correlation was used throughout for robustness to outlier
images and bounded values.  Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for
paired comparisons of bounded variables.  No multiple comparisons
correction was applied to the two pre-specified behavioural state tests
(pupil CV and running CV), which are independent by construction.  All
bootstrap confidence intervals used 1000 iterations.  The global random
seed was set to 42 and passed explicitly to all stochastic operations.

(\url{https://portal.brain-map.org}) and accessed using the AllenSDK
Python package.  Analysis code is available at
