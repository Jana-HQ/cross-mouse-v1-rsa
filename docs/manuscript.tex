\title{\textbf{Mouse V1 Passes Through a Transient Low-Dimensional State
of Peak Cross-Individual Representational Consistency During Natural
Image Viewing}}

% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
When different mice view the same natural image, their primary visual
cortex (V1) population responses share a measurable fraction of
representational structure.  Using millisecond-resolution representational
similarity analysis (RSA) applied to Neuropixels recordings from all
32 \textit{brain\_observatory\_1.1} sessions in the Allen Brain
Observatory, we show that this cross-individual consistency is not
static: it follows a stereotyped two-component temporal trajectory,
rising to a transient peak at approximately 100~ms post-stimulus onset
(mean Spearman $r = 0.307$) before decaying to a lower sustained floor
($r = 0.182$, 220--250~ms window).  This transient peak coincides
precisely with a collapse in the effective dimensionality of the shared
representation (participation ratio dropping from a pre-stimulus baseline
of $\approx$108 to $\approx$31.5 at 100~ms), and dimensionality and
consistency are tightly coupled across the entire post-stimulus epoch
(Spearman $r = -0.853$, $p = 3.5 \times 10^{-6}$): the moment mice
agree most is the moment the population code is most compressed.  This
transient low-dimensional state is specific to complex, naturalistic
stimuli --- static gratings produce an immediate sustained plateau with
no transient peak and a near-unity transient-to-sustained (T/S) ratio
--- and is highest in V1 itself (peak $r = 0.307$), with lower and
roughly comparable peak consistency across four extrastriate areas
(VISl $r = 0.186$, VISal $r = 0.234$, VISpm $r = 0.210$,
VISam $r = 0.238$).

To explain this transient compression, we tested whether it is
preferentially produced by fast-spiking inhibitory interneurons.
Waveform-based dissociation of putative fast-spiking (FSU,
narrow-waveform, $<$0.4~ms) and regular-spiking (RSU, broad-waveform)
units shows that FSU units produce a sharper transient than RSU units
(T/S ratio 2.30$\times$ vs.\ 1.67$\times$), and sessions with higher
FSU fractions show correspondingly sharper population-level transients
($\Delta$T/S $= +0.20$).  Optotagged PV$^+$ interneurons from
Pvalb-IRES-Cre$\times$Ai32 sessions show an even sharper transient
(T/S $= 3.59\times$) than waveform-inferred FSU units
(T/S $= 2.30\times$), consistent with partial impurity of the waveform
classification (3 of 18 optotagged PV$^+$ units had broad waveforms).
A Wilson-Cowan excitatory-inhibitory (E/I) firing rate model fit
simultaneously to RSU and FSU temporal consistency curves recovers an
inhibition-dominated connectivity ratio
($r_\mathrm{EI} = w_\mathrm{EE}/w_\mathrm{IE} = 0.608$, $< 1$),
reproduces the observed temporal profiles
($R^2_\mathrm{RSU} = 0.901$, $R^2_\mathrm{FSU} = 0.913$), and
generalises to held-out mice in five-fold cross-validation (mean test
$R^2 = 0.889 \pm 0.023$; $r_\mathrm{EI} < 1$ in every fold;
mean $r_\mathrm{EI} = 0.609 \pm 0.013$).  Together, these results show
that mouse V1 passes through a transient, inhibition-dominated
low-dimensional state during natural image viewing, and that this state
is precisely when representational structure is most shared across
individuals.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} primary visual cortex; representational
similarity analysis; cross-individual variability; fast-spiking
interneurons; Wilson-Cowan model; neural geometry; Allen Brain
Observatory

% ── Introduction ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

A central goal of systems neuroscience is to identify which features of
neural population activity are canonical --- shared across individuals of
the same species exposed to the same stimuli --- and which are
idiosyncratic, reflecting individual developmental history, anatomy, or
ongoing internal state.  In primary visual cortex, population responses
to natural images vary substantially across individuals in terms of
absolute firing rates and the identity of which neurons are active, yet
downstream circuits must extract a stable visual representation
regardless.  One quantitative handle on this problem is cross-individual
representational consistency: the degree to which different animals agree
on the relational structure of their population responses --- which
stimuli are represented as similar to which others --- even when their
absolute responses differ \citep{kriegeskorte2008}.  High cross-individual
consistency implies that a canonical representational geometry is expressed
in V1, potentially reflecting shared tuning properties that emerge from
common developmental programs and anatomical connectivity.

The geometry of V1 population representations has been characterised in
terms of dimensionality \citep{stringer2019}, the alignment of neural
manifolds across animals \citep{williams2021}, and the degree to which
response structure is predicted by deep convolutional neural network (CNN)
features \citep{yamins2016,cadena2019}.  These studies have generally
treated representational consistency as a static quantity, computed over a
broad temporal window that collapses the entire visual response into a
single point estimate.  However, visual responses in V1 are dynamically
structured: an early feedforward volley arriving approximately 40--70~ms
post-onset is followed by recurrent and feedback contributions that unfold
over the subsequent 200~ms \citep{lamme2000,girard2001}.  Whether
cross-individual agreement on representational structure is uniformly
distributed across this epoch, or concentrated in a particular temporal
component reflecting a specific circuit mechanism, is unknown.

Here we ask whether the V1 population code passes through a transient,
low-dimensional regime during the visual response, and if so, whether
that is the moment at which different animals' representations are most
alike.  A code transiently compressed into a small number of stereotyped
dimensions is, almost by construction, more likely to look similar across
individuals than a code spread across many idiosyncratic dimensions.
Cross-individual RSA consistency is therefore not only a measure of
agreement in its own right, but a sensitive, model-free readout of when
the population code is in such a compressed, canonical state.

Having established whether and when such a state occurs, we then ask
what produces it.  Fast-spiking interneurons --- the dominant subtype
of which expresses parvalbumin (PV$^+$) --- are a strong mechanistic
candidate.  PV$^+$ cells receive strong thalamic drive, fire with short
latency and high spike-timing precision \citep{swadlow2003}, and provide
powerful lateral inhibition onto pyramidal cells through perisomatic
synapses \citep{freund1996}.  The classical iceberg effect posits that
inhibition sculpts a broad excitatory input into a sharper, more
selective response \citep{somers1995,troyer1998}.  If this inhibitory
sharpening operates at the population level, fast-spiking units should
show a sharper version of any transient compression than broad-spiking
units, and a circuit model in which inhibition dominates recurrent
excitation should reproduce the observed dynamics.

We test these two questions in sequence using the Allen Brain Observatory
Visual Coding Neuropixels dataset \citep{siegle2021}, which provides
simultaneous recordings from hundreds of V1 units across 32 mice viewing
an identical set of 118 natural images.  We first apply a sliding-window
RSA approach at 10~ms temporal resolution to characterise the full
temporal trajectory of cross-individual consistency, and pair it with
participation ratio analysis to test directly whether peak consistency
coincides with minimum dimensionality.  We then turn to mechanism:
waveform-based cell-class dissociation, optotagged PV$^+$ recordings,
and a Wilson-Cowan E/I firing rate model fit and cross-validated on
held-out mice.  As mechanistic contrasts, we compare the temporal
profiles of natural scenes with static gratings and examine how
consistency varies across five visual areas.

% ── Results ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Results}

\subsection{Cross-mouse consistency in V1 is substantial, varies across
natural images, and is predicted by response sparsity}

We recorded spike times from VISp units in 32 mice viewing 118 natural
images (250~ms each, 50 repetitions per image) from the Allen Brain
Observatory \textit{brain\_observatory\_1.1} dataset \citep{siegle2021}.
For each session, units passing quality control (amplitude cutoff $< 0.1$,
presence ratio $> 0.9$, ISI violation rate $< 0.5$; $n = 14$--$110$ units
per session after filtering) were used to construct a
$118 \times n_\mathrm{units}$ trial-averaged, column-wise z-scored response
matrix.  Cross-mouse consistency was quantified using representational
similarity analysis (RSA): for each image, we computed the cosine
similarity of its population response vector to all other 117 images
within each mouse, then measured how much mice agreed on this ranking
using the mean Spearman rank correlation across all
$\binom{32}{2} = 496$ mouse pairs.

Within-mouse split-half reliability of the raw response vectors
(Spearman-Brown corrected, 20 random splits) was near unity
(mean $r = 0.978$, range $[0.968, 0.983]$; Figure~\ref{fig:consistency}A),
confirming that the analysis is not power-limited by within-session
trial noise.  This response-vector reliability is not directly comparable
to cross-mouse consistency, however, since the two are computed in
different spaces: reliability here is a per-unit response-vector
correlation, whereas consistency is computed on image~$\times$~image
similarity structure (RDM space).  Repeating the split-half procedure in
RDM space --- building two similarity matrices from independent trial
halves and correlating them exactly as cross-mouse consistency
correlates two mice's similarity matrices --- gave a within-mouse
RDM-space reliability of mean $r = 0.832$ (range $[0.609, 0.929]$),
which is the number properly comparable to the consistency values
reported below.

Cross-mouse consistency was significantly above zero for all 118 images
(mean $r = 0.314$, range 0.142--0.588;
Figure~\ref{fig:consistency}B).  The fourfold range across images
indicates that some images drive strongly shared representations while
others are encoded more idiosyncratically.

Response sparsity --- the fraction of units responding below the
per-mouse median absolute z-score --- was an image-level predictor of
consistency (Spearman $r = -0.470$,
$p = 7.8 \times 10^{-8}$): images activating fewer units were more
consistently represented across mice.  Response magnitude was a
secondary predictor (Spearman $r = +0.276$, $p = 2.5 \times 10^{-3}$;
Figure~\ref{fig:predictors}A--B).  A ResNet50 CNN feature embedding
(ImageNet-pretrained, layer~2) also predicted consistency in
leave-one-out ridge regression using the full 512-dimensional embedding
(LOO Pearson $r = -0.496$, $p = 1.1 \times 10^{-8}$;
Figure~\ref{fig:predictors}C).  Critically, this CNN-based prediction
was not subsumed by sparsity: partialling the full-model LOO prediction
against consistency while controlling for sparsity \textit{increased}
the correlation magnitude ($r_\mathrm{partial} = -0.828$,
$p = 5.5 \times 10^{-31}$), and a nested regression comparison showed
that adding the CNN prediction to a sparsity-only model raised
$R^2$ from 0.269 to 0.453 ($\Delta R^2 = +0.184$).  Response sparsity
and CNN-based image complexity therefore each carry independent
predictive signal about cross-mouse consistency, rather than CNN
complexity acting only through its co-variation with sparsity.  Galleries
of the most and least consistently represented images confirm the
sparsity relationship qualitatively: high-consistency images tend to be
sparse, high-contrast, or dominated by a single feature, while
low-consistency images are denser and more spatially heterogeneous
(Supplementary Figure~S1).

\subsection{Cross-mouse consistency follows a stereotyped two-component
temporal trajectory}

To characterise how cross-mouse agreement evolves over the visual
response, we applied the same RSA pipeline to 25~ms sliding windows
stepped at 10~ms increments from $-50$ to $+370$~ms relative to
stimulus onset.  Pre-stimulus consistency was near zero
(mean $r = 4 \times 10^{-5}$), confirming that the measure is not
contaminated by correlated spontaneous activity or slow drift across
sessions (Figure~\ref{fig:temporal}).

Following stimulus onset, consistency rose steeply from $\sim$40~ms,
reaching its first threshold-crossing value at 50~ms and peaking at
$r = 0.307$ at 100~ms.  Consistency then decayed monotonically to a
sustained floor estimated from the 220--250~ms window
($r = 0.182 \pm 0.003$; one-sample $t$-test vs.\ zero:
$t = 94.75$, $p = 2.6 \times 10^{-6}$).  We restrict the sustained
floor estimate to the 220--250~ms epoch specifically to avoid
contamination from the next image's neural response: in the
\textit{brain\_observatory\_1.1} protocol, natural scenes are presented
back-to-back with no inter-stimulus interval, so the subsequent image's
V1 response arrives at approximately 290--300~ms (stimulus onset at
250~ms plus $\approx$40--50~ms response latency).  Data beyond 250~ms
are not interpreted as reflecting the sustained representation of the
current image.

A Gaussian-plus-offset model formally decomposed the temporal profile into
a transient component (amplitude $0.162 \pm 0.035$, peak centre
$124.7 \pm 8.1$~ms, width $\sigma = 34.2 \pm 9.2$~ms) and a sustained
offset ($0.149 \pm 0.015$; Figure~\ref{fig:temporal}).  The fitted
Gaussian centre lags the empirical peak (100~ms) by $\sim$25~ms because
the transient rises faster than it decays, an asymmetry a symmetric
Gaussian cannot capture exactly; we report the empirical 100~ms peak
elsewhere in the text as the more direct descriptive statistic.  A coarser
comparison of early (0--75~ms) and late (75--250~ms) windows confirmed
that late-window consistency substantially exceeds early-window
consistency (mean $r = 0.084$ vs.\ $0.320$; Wilcoxon signed-rank
$p = 4.2 \times 10^{-21}$; Supplementary Figure~S3), while image rankings
were partially preserved across epochs (Spearman $r = 0.652$ between
the 100~ms and 250~ms per-image consistency vectors; Supplementary
Figure~S7).

\subsection{The transient peak coincides with a collapse of the shared
representation into a low-dimensional attractor}

To characterise the geometry of the shared representation at each time
point, we computed the participation ratio (PR) of the average cosine
similarity matrix across mice:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{PR} = \frac{\bigl(\sum_i \lambda_i\bigr)^2}{\sum_i \lambda_i^2},
  \label{eq:PR}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_i$ are the non-negative eigenvalues of the average
similarity matrix.  PR measures effective dimensionality: PR~$= 1$ if one
eigenvalue dominates; PR~$= n$ if all eigenvalues are equal.

Pre-stimulus PR of the cross-mouse-averaged similarity matrix was
$\approx 108$ --- close to the maximum of 118 given the number of
images --- indicating that spontaneous activity produces an
approximately uniform representational geometry with no shared structure
across mice.  Because averaging similarity matrices across mice pushes
disagreement toward uniformity, this averaged-matrix PR is partly
definitional with cross-mouse consistency; a less confounded view is
the PR of each mouse's own similarity matrix, averaged after the fact
(pre-stimulus per-mouse PR $\approx 35.6$), which never involves
cross-mouse averaging.  At the consistency peak (100~ms window), the
averaged-matrix PR dropped to 31.5, meaning the shared representation
occupied only $\approx$27\% of the available dimensions; the per-mouse
PR dropped correspondingly, from $\approx 35.6$ pre-stimulus to 15.3 at
the peak.  PR then partially recovered to 61.2 (averaged-matrix) and
24.4 (per-mouse) at 250~ms, tracking the decay of consistency
(Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}A).  Across all post-stimulus windows, PR and
mean consistency were strongly negatively correlated both for the
averaged-matrix PR (Spearman $r = -0.853$, $p = 3.5 \times 10^{-6}$) and,
essentially as strongly, for the less-confounded per-mouse PR
(Spearman $r = -0.805$, $p = 3.2 \times 10^{-5}$): the moment of maximal
cross-mouse agreement is precisely the moment of maximal representational
compression, and this holds even when the compression is measured
entirely within individual mice.

Eigendecomposition of the average similarity matrix at 100~ms revealed
that the top 8 eigenvectors account for 35.2\% of variance, with a
gradually decaying spectrum (14.75\%, 4.60\%, 3.57\%, 3.21\%, 2.77\%,
2.12\%, 2.07\%, 2.07\% for dimensions 1--8; Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}B).
The two dominant dimensions were interpretable in terms of low-level
image statistics: Dim~1 correlated positively with response magnitude
(Spearman $r = +0.44$) and early CNN feature strength
(layer~1: $r = +0.41$; layer~4: $r = -0.36$), representing a
signal-strength axis; Dim~2 correlated with response sparsity
($r = -0.51$), representing a spatial-scale axis (see Supplementary
Figure~S5 for the full property heatmap).  Consistent with the
distributed nature of the shared structure, overall cross-mouse
consistency did not strongly load onto any single eigenvector
(maximum $|r| = 0.28$; Figure~\ref{fig:geometry}C).  A second-order RSA
--- correlating each mouse's full representational dynamics profile
(similarity to all other images across five time windows) between mouse
pairs --- recovered a mean second-order $r = 0.187$ across images and a
temporal peak at exactly 100~ms.  This is a related but distinct
consistency statistic rather than an independent confirmation: it
correlates with first-order consistency at $r = 0.950$ across images
(Supplementary Figure~S8C), so it is better read as a re-parameterisation
that tracks the same underlying signal than as an orthogonal check.

\subsection{Fast-spiking interneurons drive the transient component of
the shared attractor}

To test whether the transient and sustained components of cross-mouse
consistency are differentially produced by distinct cell classes, we
classified VISp units by waveform trough-to-peak duration: units with
duration $< 0.4$~ms were classified as putative fast-spiking (FSU;
19.6\% of QC-passing units, mean 12.9 units per session) and units with
duration $\geq 0.4$~ms as regular-spiking (RSU; mean 53.1 units per
session).  We then computed cross-mouse consistency separately for each
subpopulation at each sliding-window time point (minimum 8 units per
session per class; FSU: 27/32 sessions; RSU: 32/32 sessions).

\subsubsection{Waveform-based dissociation}

RSU and FSU subpopulations rose with identical onset latency ($\sim$40~ms),
ruling out a temporal-precedence account of the cell-class difference
(Figure~\ref{fig:celltypes}A).  FSU units showed a higher transient peak
($r = 0.273$) than RSU units ($r = 0.246$) and a lower sustained floor
($r = 0.119$ vs.\ $0.147$), yielding a substantially sharper T/S ratio:
$2.30\times$ for FSU vs.\ $1.67\times$ for RSU (Figure~\ref{fig:celltypes}B).
When all units were combined, cross-mouse consistency exceeded either
subpopulation alone at the peak, suggesting superadditive contributions
from the two cell classes encoding complementary image dimensions.

\subsubsection{Session-level FSU fraction}

To test whether between-session variation in FSU abundance predicted the
sharpness of the population-level transient, we split sessions at the
median FSU fraction into high (mean fraction 0.285, $n = 16$) and low
(mean fraction 0.166, $n = 16$) groups (Figure~\ref{fig:celltypes}C).
The high-FSU group showed a T/S ratio of $1.81\times$ vs.\ $1.61\times$
for the low-FSU group (difference $+0.20$), with the high-FSU group
peaking more sharply and decaying faster, consistent with FSU units being
the primary driver of the transient component.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Optotagged PV$^+$ validation and the limits of waveform
classification}{Optotagged PV+ validation and the limits of waveform classification}}

To directly validate the waveform-based FSU classification and characterise
the T/S behaviour of genetically confirmed PV$^+$ units, we identified
optotagged interneurons from the five Pvalb-IRES-Cre/wt$\times$Ai32
sessions in the \textit{brain\_observatory\_1.1} dataset.  Units were
classified as optotagged PV$^+$ if they met two criteria simultaneously:
mean spike count in the 0--5~ms post-pulse window $> 0.05$ spikes per
pulse, and light-to-baseline ratio $> 2.0$ (where baseline is the 5~ms
pre-pulse window).  The 5~ms response window was chosen to exclude
polysynaptic responses.

This procedure yielded 18 optotagged VISp units across five sessions.
Three sessions contributed $\geq 3$ optotagged units (session IDs:
721123822, 760345702, 797828357; contributing 3, 3, and 10 confirmed
PV$^+$ units respectively) and were included in the cross-mouse RSA;
two sessions contributed only one unit each and were excluded.

A critical complication emerged from the waveform durations of the
optotagged units: while 15 of 18 had trough-to-peak durations $< 0.4$~ms,
three units had durations of 0.536, 0.577, and 0.618~ms (two from session
760345702 and one from session 773418906), all above the FSU threshold.
Session 773418906 contributed only this one optotagged unit and was
excluded from the cross-mouse RSA (see below), so it does not appear in
the optotagged temporal curves; its unit is counted here only for the
waveform-duration tally.  This indicates that the 0.4~ms threshold is
not a pure separator: the waveform-inferred FSU category is enriched for,
but not identical to, the confirmed PV$^+$ population.  Quantitatively,
this contamination deflates the FSU T/S ratio (by including broad-waveform
PV$^+$ cells in the RSU category) and inflates the RSU T/S ratio.

This prediction is borne out: confirmed PV$^+$ units show a sharper
transient (T/S $= 3.59\times$) than waveform-inferred FSU units
(T/S $= 2.30\times$; Figure~\ref{fig:celltypes}D--E).  The direction
and magnitude of the effect are conserved and strengthened when the
cell-type attribution is made by genetics rather than waveform, even with
only three sessions contributing to the optotagged analysis.  The absolute
consistency values are lower in the optotagged analysis (fewer units per
session $\to$ noisier similarity matrices), but T/S is a shape property
of the normalised curve and is unaffected by overall signal level.

\subsection{A Wilson-Cowan E/I model with inhibition-dominated connectivity
reproduces the temporal profiles and generalises to held-out mice}

To test whether the observed temporal dynamics are consistent with a
minimal E/I circuit model, we fit a two-population Wilson-Cowan firing
rate model \citep{wilson1972} to the RSU (E population) and FSU
(I population) temporal consistency curves simultaneously.  Both
populations receive a shared gamma-shaped thalamic drive (onset $\sim$40~ms;
shape fixed to match the observed response latency).

The model is described by:
\begin{align}
  \tau_E \dot{E} &= -E + \sigma\!\left(r_\mathrm{EI}\, w_\mathrm{IE}\, E
    - w_\mathrm{IE}\, I + D(t)\right), \label{eq:E} \\
  \tau_I \dot{I} &= -I + \sigma\!\left(w_\mathrm{EI}\, E
    - w_\mathrm{II}\, I + 0.7\, D(t)\right), \label{eq:I}
\end{align}
where $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$ is the sigmoid activation function,
$D(t)$ is a gamma-shaped thalamic drive, $\tau_E = 20$~ms and
$\tau_I = 10$~ms are fixed at biologically motivated values, and the
five free parameters are
$\{r_\mathrm{EI}, w_\mathrm{IE}, w_\mathrm{EI}, w_\mathrm{II},
I_\mathrm{amp}\}$.  The key design choice is the explicit
reparameterisation of $r_\mathrm{EI} = w_\mathrm{EE}/w_\mathrm{IE}$ as
a free parameter (bounded $[0.01, 2.0]$): $r_\mathrm{EI} < 1$ indicates
inhibition-dominated dynamics; $r_\mathrm{EI} > 1$ indicates
recurrence-dominated dynamics.  The absolute scale of $w_\mathrm{IE}$
is a nuisance parameter not well-determined by the data; $r_\mathrm{EI}$
is the primary inference target.

Optimisation yielded $r_\mathrm{EI} = 0.608$ (not at any parameter bound;
$R^2_\mathrm{RSU} = 0.901$, $R^2_\mathrm{FSU} = 0.913$;
Figure~\ref{fig:eimodel}A).  We note that $w_\mathrm{II}$ converged to
its upper bound (8.0) in all fits; the absolute value of this parameter
is not interpretable, but $r_\mathrm{EI}$ is stable across widened
$w_\mathrm{II}$ bounds (Supplementary Figure~S9B).  Across 10 random
initialisations, 9 converged to the same inhibition-dominated solution
($r_\mathrm{EI} \approx 0.608$) while one escaped to a competing,
recurrence-dominated basin near $r_\mathrm{EI} \approx 1.01$
(mean $r_\mathrm{EI} = 0.648 \pm 0.120$ across all 10;
Supplementary Figure~S9A).  This bimodality means the inhibition-dominated
fit is the dominant but not the only attractor of the optimisation
landscape.  Sensitivity analysis confirmed that T/S
ratios $> 1$ require $r_\mathrm{EI} < 1$: increasing $r_\mathrm{EI}$
above 1 flattens the transient and produces a sustained plateau
inconsistent with the data (Figure~\ref{fig:eimodel}B).

To test whether the inhibition-dominance conclusion generalises to mice
not used in fitting, we performed five-fold cross-validation over sessions
(Figure~\ref{fig:eimodel}C).  In each fold, RSU and FSU consistency
curves were computed from the $\sim$80\% training sessions and the model
was fit using three warm starts near the full-data solution; the fitted
parameters were then used to predict curves from the held-out
$\sim$20\%.  Results: mean train $R^2 = 0.899 \pm 0.005$; mean test
$R^2 = 0.889 \pm 0.023$; $r_\mathrm{EI} = 0.609 \pm 0.013$;
$r_\mathrm{EI} < 1$ in every fold (fold-level values: 0.627, 0.614,
0.605, 0.592, 0.609).  The inhibition-dominance conclusion is robust even
in the worst-performing fold (test $R^2 = 0.871$,
$r_\mathrm{EI} = 0.592$).  Because each fold is fit with warm starts near
the full-data solution, this cross-validation demonstrates that the fit
\textit{quality} generalises to held-out mice; it does not by itself
demonstrate that $r_\mathrm{EI}$ is basin-independent within each fold ---
that evidence comes instead from the 10-random-initialisation stability
analysis above.

\subsection{Controls: adaptation and behavioural state do not explain
the results}

\paragraph{Adaptation.}
If all mice habituate to the same images in the same way, their responses
might converge over the session through shared adaptation rather than
shared tuning.  To test this, we split each session's trials by temporal
order and computed cross-mouse consistency from the chronologically first
and second halves separately.  Per-image consistency was highly stable
across halves (Spearman $r = 0.960$, $p = 6.1 \times 10^{-66}$;
Figure~\ref{fig:controls}A), and both halves correlated with full-session
consistency at $r > 0.984$ (Supplementary Figure~S10B--C).  The mean
per-image difference (early $-$ late consistency) was a small but
statistically significant $+0.015$ (one-sample $t$-test
$p = 1.1 \times 10^{-8}$) --- in the direction opposite to a shared
adaptation confound, which would predict convergence in the second half
--- consistent instead with modest non-selective neural fatigue that
attenuates all images equally without distorting representational
structure.

\paragraph{Behavioural state.}
If mice happen to be in more similar arousal or locomotion states during
certain images, those images would appear more consistently represented
for reasons unrelated to their visual content.  The coefficient of
variation (CV) of pupil area across mice per image was not significantly
correlated with per-image consistency (Spearman $r = +0.056$, $p = 0.55$,
$n = 26$ sessions with eye-tracking; Figure~\ref{fig:controls}B).  The CV
of running speed was likewise uncorrelated ($r = -0.033$, $p = 0.72$,
$n = 32$ sessions; Figure~\ref{fig:controls}C).  The narrow range of CV
across images (pupil CV SD $\approx 0.014$, running CV SD
$\approx 0.026$) limits statistical power for both tests, and
particularly for the pupil test given its smaller session count
($n = 26$); with this little variation to correlate against, the tests
are underpowered to detect anything but a fairly large true effect, so
the null result is interpreted cautiously rather than as positive
evidence of no relationship.

\subsection{The transient is specific to natural scenes: gratings produce
an immediate sustained plateau}

Static gratings produced substantially higher absolute consistency than
natural scenes (mean $r = 0.720$ vs.\ $r = 0.183$ for natural scenes in
the fixed 0--250~ms window; Figure~\ref{fig:gratings}), but the temporal
structure differed qualitatively.  Both stimulus classes have similarly
high within-mouse response reliability (gratings mean $= 0.992$, natural
scenes mean $= 0.978$; Methods), so this comparison is not confounded by
differential reliability between conditions.  Gratings rose by
$\sim$60~ms and then remained flat for the rest of the 0--250~ms epoch
rather than resolving into a clear plateau-then-hold: the quoted peak
($r = 0.680$) falls at the 250~ms window edge, where the curve is still
drifting slightly upward, giving a near-unity T/S ratio (approximately
$1.00\times$).  Natural scenes showed the characteristic
transient-plus-sustained structure (peak $r = 0.307$ at 100~ms,
T/S $= 1.68\times$).  We restrict quantitative comparisons to the
0--250~ms window for the same contamination reason as natural scenes.

This comparison is consistent with the view that feedforward responses
to simple, low-complexity stimuli are sufficient to produce stable shared
representations in V1, while the complex stimulus-specific inhibitory
sharpening mechanism is only recruited for natural images that engage a
broader, less stereotyped neural population.

\subsection{Cross-area hierarchy: consistency and onset latency vary with
hierarchical position}

We applied the same sliding-window pipeline to four additional visual
areas (VISl, VISal, VISpm, VISam; session counts 24, 23, 20, 26
respectively, with $\geq 10$ QC-passing units per session).  VISp had the
highest peak consistency ($r = 0.307$) and earliest onset (50~ms, peak at
100~ms).  The four extrastriate areas showed later onsets and lower,
roughly comparable peak consistency rather than a monotonic decline with
hierarchical position: VISl $r = 0.186$ (onset 60~ms, peak 110~ms),
VISal $r = 0.234$ (onset 60~ms, peak 100~ms), VISpm $r = 0.210$
(onset 70~ms, peak 120~ms), VISam $r = 0.238$ (onset 70~ms, peak 110~ms;
Figure~\ref{fig:hierarchy}).  All five areas have similarly high
within-mouse response reliability (range 0.964--0.978; Methods), so
this ordering reflects genuine differences in raw consistency rather
than differential reliability.  The transient-plus-sustained temporal
structure was qualitatively present in all areas.  Between-area
comparisons are reported descriptively; areas share sessions, and a
formal test would require a mixed model accounting for this dependence.

% ── Discussion ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}

We have shown that mouse V1 does not represent natural images with a
constant degree of cross-individual agreement.  Instead, it passes through
a transient, low-dimensional population state --- peaking at $\sim$100~ms
post-stimulus and visible directly in the participation ratio of the
shared representation --- and this transient state is precisely the moment
at which different animals' representations are most alike.  Three further
lines of evidence indicate that this transient compression is produced by
fast-spiking inhibitory interneurons acting through inhibition-dominated
feedforward dynamics.

\subsection{Summary of convergent evidence}

The two most direct findings require no cell-type classification, no
circuit model, and no auxiliary assumptions.  First, the temporal profile
of cross-individual consistency follows a stereotyped
transient-plus-sustained shape that is incompatible with purely feedforward
tuning (which would produce a step function) or purely recurrent attractor
dynamics (which would maintain a plateau).  Second, this transient peak
coincides precisely with a collapse of the shared representational geometry
to its minimum dimensionality (PR~$= 31.5$ at 100~ms, vs.\ a pre-stimulus
baseline of $\approx 108$, for the cross-mouse-averaged similarity
matrix), and PR and consistency are tightly coupled across the entire
post-stimulus epoch (Spearman $r = -0.853$).  The same coupling holds,
essentially as strongly, when PR is computed independently within each
mouse and only then averaged (per-mouse PR: pre-stimulus $\approx 35.6 \to$
peak 15.3; Spearman $r = -0.805$), which rules out the concern that the
averaged-matrix result is merely a restatement of consistency itself.
These two
findings are measured directly from the same similarity matrices and are,
in effect, two views of a single underlying phenomenon: cross-individual
agreement is high exactly when, and because, the population code is
compressed into a small number of stereotyped dimensions.  This coupling
is the strongest and most direct result in the paper, and it does not
depend on any of the mechanistic claims that follow.

The remaining results address \textit{why} this compression occurs.
Third, FSU units produce a sharper transient than RSU units despite
identical onset latency, and sessions with more FSU units show
correspondingly sharper population-level transients.  Fourth, genetically
confirmed PV$^+$ units show an even sharper transient
(T/S $= 3.59\times$) than waveform-inferred FSU units
(T/S $= 2.30\times$), strengthening the cell-type attribution while
revealing an impurity in the waveform classification.  Fifth, a
five-parameter Wilson-Cowan E/I model fit to training sessions generalises
to held-out mice with mean test $R^2 = 0.889$ and recovers an
inhibition-dominated ratio ($r_\mathrm{EI} = 0.609 \pm 0.013$) stable
across all five cross-validation folds.

Among the mechanistic evidence, the cross-validated E/I model is the
strongest single piece: a minimal circuit description fit to $\sim$26
sessions reproduces the temporal dynamics of the remaining $\sim$6 with
test $R^2 > 0.87$ in every fold, and the inhibition-dominance conclusion
is robust even in the worst fold.  The optotagging result, despite the
small sample ($n = 3$ usable sessions), is the most direct evidence for
the PV$^+$ attribution specifically.  We emphasise, however, that the
central empirical claim --- the existence and timing of the transient
low-dimensional state, and its coupling to cross-individual consistency
--- does not depend on the correctness of the circuit-level
interpretation.

\subsection{Relationship to prior work}

The high-dimensional spontaneous geometry of V1 (pre-stimulus per-mouse
PR~$\approx 35.6$) is consistent with \citet{stringer2019}, who
characterised the within-animal dimensionality of V1 population
responses as a high-dimensional power-law eigenspectrum; the per-mouse
PR, rather than the cross-mouse-averaged-matrix PR ($\approx 108$), is
the quantity comparable to their within-animal measurements, since
averaging similarity matrices across mice inflates apparent
dimensionality by washing out cross-mouse disagreement.  Our contribution
is the demonstration that stimulus onset drives a rapid collapse in both
the averaged-matrix PR (to $\approx 31.5$) and the per-mouse PR (to
$\approx 15.3$) --- a low-dimensional attractor by either measure --- and
that this collapse is shared across individuals.  The
subsequent partial recovery (PR $\to 61.2$ by 250~ms) suggests that the
sustained representation is less structured than the transient peak,
consistent with the smaller sustained consistency floor.

Our RSA approach differs from Procrustes alignment \citep{williams2021}
and hyperalignment \citep{haxby2011} in that it measures agreement on
representational \textit{structure} --- which stimuli are similar to which
others --- rather than the orientation of neural axes, making it invariant
to individual differences in which neurons are tuned to which features.
The mean consistency of $r = 0.314$, against a within-mouse RDM-space
reliability of $r = 0.832$ (Methods), indicates that a substantial share
of the available representational signal is shared across individuals,
with the remainder reflecting idiosyncratic tuning or developmental
variation.

The feedforward/recurrent distinction proposed by \citet{lamme2000} maps
naturally onto our two-component temporal profile: the transient at
$\sim$100~ms falls within the early feedforward epoch, while the sustained
floor likely reflects contributions from recurrent intra-areal and
inter-areal dynamics.  The fact that cross-mouse agreement is maximal
during the feedforward component is consistent with shared thalamic-driven
tuning being more stereotyped across individuals than recurrent
contributions shaped by individual experience and circuit variation.

\subsection{The optotagging complication and its interpretation}

The finding that 3 of 18 optotagged PV$^+$ units had waveform durations
at or above the canonical 0.4~ms threshold is an important caveat for
waveform-based cell-type inference.  This result is not unprecedented:
prior work has noted that a subset of PV$^+$ cells express intermediate
waveform widths, particularly in superficial cortical layers
\citep{bartho2004,mccormick1985}.  The practical consequence for our
analysis is quantifiable: contamination of the RSU pool by misclassified
PV$^+$ cells inflates the RSU T/S ratio slightly and deflates the FSU
T/S ratio, producing exactly the pattern we observe
(optotagged T/S $3.59\times$ $>$ waveform FSU T/S $2.30\times$).  The
optotagging result therefore not only validates the direction of the
waveform-based finding but also provides a corrected, higher estimate of
the true PV$^+$ T/S ratio.  Our main conclusions are
\textit{strengthened} rather than weakened by this complication.

\subsection{Limitations}

\paragraph{E/I model identifiability.}
The Wilson-Cowan model is a phenomenological description, not a
mechanistic claim.  The $w_\mathrm{II}$ parameter converged to its upper
bound in all fits, indicating that self-inhibition among FSU units is
poorly determined by these data; its absolute value should not be
interpreted.  The key inference target $r_\mathrm{EI}$ is not at any
bound and is stable across wider parameter ranges
(Supplementary Figure~S9B).  The 10-random-initialisation stability check
also reveals a competing, recurrence-dominated optimum
($r_\mathrm{EI} \approx 1.01$) that one of ten restarts converges to; the
inhibition-dominated solution is the dominant basin (9/10 restarts) but
not the only one, so this should be read as a strong majority result
rather than a globally unique optimum.  More constrained inference would
require single-trial data or perturbational experiments that
independently vary E and I drive.

\paragraph{Sustained floor interpretation.}
The sustained component ($r = 0.182$, 220--250~ms) is estimated from a
narrow window forced by the back-to-back presentation protocol.  Whether
it reflects a genuine attractor state, residual stimulus-driven activity,
or early contributions from the subsequent image cannot be fully resolved.
A design with variable inter-stimulus intervals would be necessary to
distinguish these possibilities.

\paragraph{Optotagging sample size.}
The optotagged PV$^+$ analysis rests on only three sessions contributing
3, 3, and 10 confirmed units respectively.  The T/S estimate should be
treated as directional support for the waveform-based finding, not as a
precise quantitative estimate of the true PV$^+$ T/S ratio.

\paragraph{Cross-area comparisons.}
Areas share sessions, so between-area comparisons of consistency levels
are not statistically independent.  The observed pattern --- highest in
VISp, lower and roughly comparable across the four extrastriate areas
--- is consistent with known hierarchical properties of the mouse visual
system but should be interpreted descriptively pending a formal
mixed-model analysis.

\paragraph{Attractor geometry and image content.}
The two dominant attractor dimensions account for only 19.3\% of the
shared variance.  The remaining shared structure is not accounted for by
the low-level image statistics and CNN features examined here, and may
reflect higher-level image properties or individual-animal variation not
captured by our cross-mouse RSA measure.

\subsection{Broader significance}

These findings establish that the transient compression of the V1
population code is a measurable, reproducible property of the visual
response --- and, in our data, a mechanistically interpretable one.  The
cross-validated generalisability of the E/I model implies that the circuit
mechanism producing this trajectory is not merely a feature of the 32 mice
sampled but a stable property of the mouse V1 architecture.

More broadly, if the shared geometry of V1 is transiently imposed by
inhibitory interneurons during a 50--150~ms window after stimulus onset,
then downstream areas reading out V1 activity during this window receive
a cleaner, more stereotyped signal than at other times.  This temporal
gating of canonical representational structure may be a general principle
of cortical computation, extending beyond V1 to other sensory and
association areas where fast-spiking interneurons play similar roles in
shaping population dynamics.  The finding that the transient is absent for
static gratings and lower in extrastriate areas relative to VISp further
suggests that this mechanism is specifically engaged by complex,
naturalistic stimuli that require active representational compression,
though the drop from VISp to the four extrastriate areas does not itself
follow a graded, step-by-step pattern.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Dataset}

All data were obtained from the Allen Brain Observatory Visual Coding
Neuropixels dataset \citep{siegle2021}, accessed via the AllenSDK
(version~2.x).  We used all 32 sessions of type
\textit{brain\_observatory\_1.1}, in which head-fixed mice passively
viewed a standardised battery of natural and artificial stimuli while six
Neuropixels probes recorded simultaneously from multiple visual areas.
The natural scenes stimulus (118 greyscale images, 250~ms per image, 50
repetitions per image, back-to-back with no inter-stimulus interval) and
the static gratings stimulus (orientation $\times$ spatial-frequency
conditions, 250~ms per presentation) were used.  All analyses used the
VISp area designation from the Allen Common Coordinate Framework~v3 for
the main analysis; the cross-area analysis additionally included VISl,
VISal, VISpm, and VISam.  In the \textit{brain\_observatory\_1.1} dataset
there is one session per mouse, so ``32 mice'' and ``32 sessions'' are
used interchangeably throughout, and sessions are the resampling/held-out
unit in the cluster bootstrap and cross-validation procedures described
below.

\subsection{Unit quality control}

Units were retained if they met all three Allen Brain Observatory
recommended quality criteria: amplitude cutoff $< 0.1$, presence ratio
$> 0.9$, and ISI violation rate $< 0.5$.  Sessions were included in the
analysis of a given area only if they contributed at least 10 QC-passing
units in that area.  All 32 sessions contributed at least 10 VISp units
(range: 14--110).  Session counts for other areas were: VISl 24,
VISal 23, VISpm 20, VISam 26.

\subsection{Preprocessing and response matrix construction}

Spike times were binned into 1~ms bins spanning $-75$ to $+404$~ms
relative to stimulus onset and stored as a tensor of shape
(images $\times$ units $\times$ bins) per session.  Any response window
$[t_\mathrm{lo}, t_\mathrm{hi})$~ms was reconstructed by summing the
relevant bin columns, then z-scoring per unit (across images).  This
single-pass preprocessing avoids redundant NWB reads across analyses.
For the main 0--250~ms analysis, z-scored matrices were computed
directly from the summed tensor columns.

\subsection{Within-mouse reliability}

Within-mouse split-half reliability was estimated by randomly partitioning
each image's 50 trials into two halves, computing the mean population
response vector for each half, and measuring their Spearman rank
correlation.  This was repeated 20 times and averaged.  The Spearman-Brown
formula $r_\mathrm{SB} = 2r/(1+r)$ estimated full-data reliability.  This
response-vector reliability (mean of $r_\mathrm{SB}$ across images and
mice) is a QC diagnostic confirming the analysis is not power-limited by
within-session trial noise, but it is not in the same space as
cross-mouse consistency and should not be used to normalise it: it is
computed from raw per-unit response vectors, whereas consistency is
computed on image~$\times$~image similarity structure (RDM space).

To obtain a reliability estimate genuinely comparable to consistency, we
additionally computed an RDM-space split-half reliability for VISp
natural scenes, the one area/stimulus combination for which per-trial
spike data was available.  Each mouse's trials were split in half (20
random splits), each half was converted to a response matrix and then to
a cosine similarity matrix (identical to the main RSA pipeline below),
and the two resulting per-mouse similarity matrices were Spearman
rank-correlated against each other exactly as \texttt{per\_image\_consistency}
correlates two different mice's similarity matrices, then Spearman-Brown
corrected and averaged over splits and mice.  This RDM-space reliability
(mean $r = 0.832$) is the number reported alongside consistency values in
Results.  It was not computed for the four extrastriate areas or for
static gratings because per-trial spike data for those conditions was not
retained in the cached preprocessing archive; area and stimulus-type
comparisons therefore use raw consistency values throughout rather than a
normalised percentage, since both response-vector reliability (0.964--0.992
across all areas and stimulus types) and, where computable, RDM-space
reliability are high and broadly comparable across the conditions being
compared.

\subsection{Representational similarity analysis and cross-mouse
consistency}

For each session, the inter-image cosine similarity matrix was computed
from the z-scored response matrix:
$S_{ij} = \mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_j /
(\|\mathbf{x}_i\| \|\mathbf{x}_j\| + \epsilon)$,
$\epsilon = 10^{-8}$.  Per-image cross-mouse consistency was the mean
Spearman rank correlation, across all mouse pairs $(a,b)$, between their
row-wise similarity profiles for each image.

Cluster bootstrap confidence intervals resampled whole mice (not pairs)
with replacement for 1000 iterations.  When a mouse appeared multiple
times in a bootstrap sample, pairs consisting of two copies of the same
original mouse were excluded, preventing spurious self-correlation from
inflating bootstrap consistency estimates.

\subsection{CNN embeddings}

ResNet50 pretrained on ImageNet (IMAGENET1K\_V2) was used as a feature
extractor; global average pooling was applied to each convolutional block
to yield a fixed-length embedding per image.  Ridge regression with
leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO, 118 images) was used because the
feature dimension (256--2048) greatly exceeds the number of images.
Partial Spearman correlation used rank residualisation: both $x$ and $y$
were rank-transformed, then the linear effect of the rank-transformed
covariate was regressed out before computing the correlation of the
residuals.  When testing whether CNN-based prediction is independent of
sparsity, the partial correlation and nested-regression comparison
($R^2$ for consistency~$\sim$~sparsity alone vs.\ consistency~$\sim$~sparsity
$+$ CNN) were computed against the full ridge LOO prediction from the
512-dimensional layer~2 embedding, not against a scalar summary of the
embedding, since it is the full-model prediction that carries the
predictive signal being tested for independence.

\subsection{Sliding-window temporal analysis}

The RSA pipeline was repeated in 25~ms windows stepped at 10~ms
increments from $-50$ to $+370$~ms, reconstructed from the binned tensor.
Response matrices were z-scored independently within each window.  The
sustained floor was estimated from the 220--250~ms window only, to avoid
contamination from the subsequent image's V1 response.  The onset
threshold was defined as the first window where mean consistency exceeded
$\max(2 \times \text{pre-stimulus baseline},\, 0.05)$.

\subsection{Participation ratio}

The PR of a positive semi-definite matrix $\mathbf{M}$ with eigenvalues
$\lambda_i \geq 0$ is $\mathrm{PR}(\mathbf{M}) =
(\sum_i \lambda_i)^2 / \sum_i \lambda_i^2$.  At each time window,
the average similarity matrix was formed by summing per-session cosine
similarity matrices and dividing by the number of sessions.

\subsection{Waveform-based cell class classification}

Units were classified as FSU (fast-spiking, putative PV$^+$) if their
trough-to-peak waveform duration was $< 0.4$~ms, and as RSU
(regular-spiking, putative pyramidal/excitatory) otherwise, using the
canonical threshold \citep{bartho2004,mccormick1985}.  Sessions with
fewer than 8 QC-passing units of a given class were excluded from that
class's analysis (FSU: 27/32 sessions retained; RSU: 32/32).

\subsection{Optotagging validation}

Genetically confirmed PV$^+$ units were identified from the five
Pvalb-IRES-Cre/wt$\times$Ai32(RCL-ChR2(H134R)\_EYFP)/wt sessions in
the \textit{brain\_observatory\_1.1} dataset.  Single light pulse epochs
were used.  QC-passing VISp units were classified as optotagged PV$^+$
if: (i)~mean spike count in the 0--5~ms post-pulse window $> 0.05$ spikes
per pulse; and (ii)~light-to-baseline ratio $> 2.0$, where baseline was
the mean spike count in the 5~ms pre-pulse window.  The 5~ms response
window excludes polysynaptic responses (pyramidal cells driven by PV$^+$
firing respond at 7--15~ms latency).  Sessions with fewer than 3
optotagged VISp units were excluded from the cross-mouse RSA (sessions
746083955 and 773418906 contributed one unit each).

\subsection{E/I model}

The two-population Wilson-Cowan model \citep{wilson1972} is described by
equations~\eqref{eq:E}--\eqref{eq:I}.  The thalamic drive $D(t)$ is a
gamma-shaped function (shape parameter $k = 5$, scale $\theta = 14$~ms)
with onset at 40~ms, normalised to unit amplitude.  Time constants were
fixed at $\tau_E = 20$~ms and $\tau_I = 10$~ms.  The five free
parameters are $\{r_\mathrm{EI}, w_\mathrm{IE}, w_\mathrm{EI},
w_\mathrm{II}, I_\mathrm{amp}\}$ with bounds $r_\mathrm{EI} \in
[0.01, 2.0]$, $w_\mathrm{IE} \in [0.1, 20.0]$,
$w_\mathrm{EI} \in [0.1, 15.0]$, $w_\mathrm{II} \in [0.0, 8.0]$,
$I_\mathrm{amp} \in [0.5, 20.0]$.  The model was integrated with
\texttt{scipy.integrate.odeint} (rtol $= 10^{-6}$, atol $= 10^{-8}$)
and fit by minimising the sum of mean squared errors between normalised
model outputs and normalised RSU/FSU consistency curves simultaneously
using L-BFGS-B.  Sensitivity to the $w_\mathrm{II}$ upper bound is shown
in Supplementary Figure~S9B.

\subsection{Cross-validation}

Five-fold cross-validation was performed over sessions.  In each fold,
RSU and FSU temporal consistency curves were computed from the
$\sim$80\% training sessions; the model was fit using three warm starts
near the full-data solution.  The fitted parameters were then used to
predict curves from the $\sim$20\% held-out sessions.  Train and test
$R^2$ were computed on amplitude-normalised curves.

\subsection{Grating response matrices}

Grating conditions were defined as unique (orientation, spatial frequency)
pairs; only conditions present in all 32 sessions (intersection:
30~conditions) were retained.  The same QC thresholds, z-scoring
procedure, and RSA pipeline were applied as for natural scenes.

\subsection{Statistical tests}

Spearman rank correlation was used throughout for robustness to outlier
images and bounded values.  Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for
paired comparisons of bounded variables.  No multiple comparisons
correction was applied to the two pre-specified behavioural state tests
(pupil CV and running CV), which are independent by construction.  All
bootstrap confidence intervals used 1000 iterations.  The global random
seed was set to 42 and passed explicitly to all stochastic operations.

\subsection{Data and code availability}

All neural data are publicly available through the Allen Brain Observatory
(\url{https://portal.brain-map.org}) and accessed using the AllenSDK
Python package.  Analysis code is available at
\url{https://github.com/Jana-HQ/cross-mouse-v1-rsa}.
